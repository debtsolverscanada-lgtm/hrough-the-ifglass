Glint has ninety seconds.

Somewhere below him, on the cold floor of the museum basement, Nan is fighting for breath. Her heart is fluttering like a trapped bird. The paramedics are inside the building. But they're lost in the dark. And they can't see the one person who knows the way.

Nobody can.

Because Glint is invisible.

All except two tiny dark dots, floating in the air where his eyes should be.

<break time="1.0s" />

Let's go back. Three days.

It's two in the morning at the city science museum, and Glint is alone with the newest exhibit. A giant glass lens, wider than a door, built to show how light bends. A storm is rattling the skylights. He leans in close to polish a smudge.

Lightning hits the roof.

The hall flashes white. The lens hums. And the light doesn't bounce off Glint anymore. It goes straight through him.

He looks down at his hands. There are no hands. No sneakers. No mint scarf. Just floor tiles, perfectly clear, where his body should be.

He runs to the bathroom mirror. Nothing. Then he leans closer. And he sees them. Two small dark circles, hanging in midair.

His eyes.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules. And they come from real science.

You see an object because light bounces off it, or bends as it passes through. A glass of water is see-through, but you can still spot it, because light bends at its edges. To be truly invisible, every part of you would have to treat light exactly the way air does. Nothing bounces. Nothing bends.

But there's a catch. To see, the back of your eye, your retina, has to catch light. It has to absorb it. That's how its cells turn light into signals for your brain. A perfectly invisible eye would let all the light pass straight through.

A truly invisible person would be blind.

So in this world, the rule is this. Everything about Glint turns invisible, including anything he holds. Everything except the light-catching layers at the back of his eyes. Two dark dots.

That's the price of seeing.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Breakfast.

Nan is baking. She bakes when she's worried, and Glint didn't come home last night. A tray of cinnamon scones is cooling by the window. One scone lifts into the air... and vanishes.

Nan freezes. Then she says, "Glint. Put. It. Back."

He laughs. She shrieks. Mr. Pickles, the grumpy old cat, stares at two floating dots and refuses to blink. Nan sits down hard, one hand on her chest. Her heart is weak. Glint promises to be careful. He means it.

Minus: His own hands.

You have a hidden sense called proprioception. It tells your brain where your arms and legs are, even with your eyes shut. Glint still has it. He can touch his nose in the dark. But your brain also leans on sight to guide your fingers, for anything fiddly. Try buttering toast with hands you can't see. He butters the cat.

Plus: Kai.

Kai's first idea is to haunt their old school. His second idea is to haunt the mayor. His third idea is a ghost video at the museum. Glint says no three times. So Kai just snaps a flash photo of him in the dark hall.

On the screen, two glowing red dots.

It's the red-eye effect. A camera flash shines straight into an eye, bounces off the blood-rich layers at the back, and comes straight back to the lens. Kai posts it. "The Museum Ghost" has two million views by lunchtime.

Minus: The street.

Being invisible means nobody sees you coming. Including drivers. A cyclist nearly flattens him on a crossing. A bus mirror clips his shoulder. The city was not built for someone who isn't there.

Plus: Dr. Osei's lab.

Dr. Osei stays calm, as always, and points a heat camera at him. There he is. Glowing orange. In this world, the power only hides him from the light our eyes can see. His body still gives off heat, as infrared light, just like yours does.

Then Dr. Osei shows him a picture of a real glass frog. These little frogs turn far more see-through when they sleep, by packing most of their red blood cells into their liver. But even a glass frog can't hide its eyes.

Dr. Osei says, "Nature always leaves the eyes."

Minus: Nan stops sleeping.

She can't see his face. She can't tell when he's tired, or sad, or fibbing. Nan says, "I raised you by looking at you."

So on the third night, in another storm, she does something stubborn. She takes the late bus to the museum with a flask of soup, to check on him. She lets herself in through the staff entrance. She takes the service stairs down to the basement.

And the power goes out.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint hears the crash from two floors up.

He finds her by the sound of her breathing. Nan is on the basement floor beside the old fossil drawers. She's awake, but pale, and her heart is racing out of rhythm. She whispers that her chest feels tight. He grabs her phone and calls an ambulance.

Ten minutes later, the paramedics are in the building. And the building is a maze.

Four floors. Hundreds of rooms. Every light is out. The storm has set off the fire alarm, and it's so loud that shouting is useless. Their flashlights swing through the dinosaur hall. The wrong way. Toward the wrong stairs.

Glint sprints to them. He waves. They see nothing. He grabs a spare flashlight to lead them, and it fades in his hand. Anything he holds turns invisible. He is a guide nobody can follow.

On the phone, the operator is clear. Nan needs help now. Not in ten minutes. Now.

Nan is a hundred and forty metres away. Down two staircases. Through three doors.

The paramedics turn back toward the exit.

Ninety seconds, Glint thinks. Maybe less.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then he remembers Kai's photo.

Two red dots. The red-eye effect.

It happens when light shines into an eye along almost the same line someone is looking from. The light bounces off the back of the eye and returns, straight toward its source. A camera flash does it. So does a flashlight, held near a face.

Glint steps right in front of the first paramedic. He bends until his eyes are level with the beam. And he looks straight down it.

Two red sparks blaze in the dark.

The paramedic stops dead. Glint takes three steps back. The sparks move. He waits. The flashlight follows.

So he walks backwards through the black museum, eyes locked on the beam, like a cat on a midnight road. Down the stairs. Through the doors. Every time the light wanders, he steps back into it.

Two red sparks, leading the way.

Forty seconds later, the paramedics are kneeling beside Nan.

<break time="1.0s" />

Nan is going to be fine. Her heart settles in the ambulance. By sunrise, she's sitting up in a hospital bed, complaining about the tea.

And by sunrise, Glint is fading back in. Hands. Scarf. Glasses.

But here's the strange part. Nan was never scared of the floating dots. Not once.

Because, she tells him, his grandfather worked nights at that museum too. In the summer of nineteen sixty-eight, during a storm, he vanished for three days. And she found him by his eyes.

Nan says, "I always know where to look."
