You walk into your garden. You snip a tomato off the vine. And from somewhere in the leaves, there's a sharp little click. Then another. And another.

That's not a sound effect. Scientists recorded it. And by the end of this video, you'll never look at a salad the same way.

Let's start with the experiment.

In twenty twenty-three, scientists in Israel put tomato and tobacco plants into soundproof boxes, surrounded by ultrasonic microphones. Some plants were healthy. Some hadn't been watered for days. And some had just had their stems cut.

The healthy plants were almost silent. Less than one sound an hour.

But the thirsty plants? They clicked about thirty-five times an hour. The cut plants clicked too. Over and over.

And these weren't tiny sounds. Up close, about ten centimeters away, they were roughly as loud as a normal conversation.

So why have you never heard one?

Because they're too high. Humans can hear up to about twenty thousand vibrations per second. The plant clicks were mostly two or three times higher than that. Ultrasonic. Just like bat calls.

When the scientists slowed the clicks down so humans could hear them, they sounded like popcorn. Like bubble wrap. Little pops in the dark.

Now let's give you the power to hear them.

You wake up, and the first thing you notice is that the room isn't silent anymore. The plant on your windowsill is ticking. Softly. Every few minutes.

Forgot to water it? Now it's telling you.

That's actually one of the most useful things the scientists found. A computer listening to the clicks could tell whether a plant was thirsty or had been cut, just from the sound. One day, farmers might put microphones in their fields and hear which plants need water before a single leaf wilts.

But you wouldn't need a computer. You'd just walk past and know.

So... are the plants actually screaming in pain?

Here's the honest answer. Probably not the way you would.

Plants don't have brains. They don't have nerves like ours. So scientists think the clicks are mechanical. A plant pulls water up from its roots through tiny tubes, like drinking through a really long straw. When the plant gets thirsty, the water is pulled so hard that air bubbles form inside those tubes... and pop.

Click.

So it's less like a scream, and more like the creaking of a ship in a storm. A sound of stress. Not a cry for help.

But here's where it gets strange. Even if the plant isn't trying to talk... something might be listening.

<break time="1.0s" />

Moths can hear ultrasound. And moths lay their eggs on plants, so their caterpillars have something to eat when they hatch. A plant that's clicking from thirst is a terrible place to raise your babies. Scientists think some insects might use those clicks to choose which plants to avoid. That hasn't been proven yet. But the information is right there, floating through the air, for anything with the right ears.

And here's the wild part. Plants react to sound too.

In one experiment, scientists played the buzz of a bee's wings to evening primrose flowers. Within three minutes, the flowers made their nectar sweeter. As if they were saying: a customer's here, better make it good. When the scientists played other sounds, the flowers didn't change.

In another study, scientists recorded the vibrations of a caterpillar chewing on a leaf. Then they played those vibrations to other plants. And those plants started making more of their defensive chemicals. Getting ready for an attack that hadn't even happened.

Pea plants have even grown their roots toward the sound of water running through a sealed pipe. Water they couldn't touch. Only hear.

Plants don't have ears. But they're not as deaf as we thought.

Now step outside.

On a hot summer afternoon, the whole street is crackling. Every thirsty hedge, every dry flower bed, clicking away like a field of popcorn. You'd hear a drought coming before the weather forecast did.

And trees do it too. Scientists have been recording clicks from thirsty trees for decades. In a heatwave, a whole forest would be crackling around you.

Then the rain comes. And slowly, the clicking fades. The garden goes calm.

And then there's the lawn mower.

You've smelled freshly cut grass. Everyone loves that smell. But that smell is the grass releasing chemicals because it's been damaged. Plants use smells like this as warning signals.

And some plants use them to call for help. When a caterpillar chews on a corn plant, the plant releases a smell that attracts tiny wasps. The wasps follow the smell, find the caterpillar... and lay their eggs inside it.

The plant called for backup. And the backup is terrifying.

So with your new power, if grass clicks like those cut tomatoes did, every lawn mower would leave a wave of popping behind it. And that summer smell you love would never feel quite the same.
