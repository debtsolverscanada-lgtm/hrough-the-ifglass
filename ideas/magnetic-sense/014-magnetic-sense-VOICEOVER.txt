What if you could feel Earth's magnetic field?

You'd always know which way is north. In a forest. In a city you've never visited. In total darkness. You'd never get lost again.

Birds do this. Sea turtles do this. And by the end of this video, you'll find out that your brain might be reacting to it right now, without you knowing.

First, what is Earth's magnetic field?

Deep under your feet, nearly three thousand kilometers down, Earth's outer core is a giant ocean of hot, liquid metal. Mostly iron. As it churns and flows, it creates a magnetic field that wraps around the whole planet.

That's why a compass needle points north. It's lining up with that field.

The field is weak. A fridge magnet is around a hundred times stronger. But it's everywhere, all the time. Invisible. And you've never felt it once.

<break time="1.0s" />

Some animals can.

European robins fly thousands of kilometers when they migrate. Often at night. They use the stars. But they also seem to use Earth's magnetic field as a compass.

And here's the strange part. Scientists think the robin's compass might be in its eyes. Special proteins in the eye may react to the magnetic field through a weird bit of quantum physics. Some researchers think birds might actually see the magnetic field. Like a faint pattern laid over everything they look at.

Remember our WiFi episode? Scientists in Germany found that weak radio noise, the kind coming from electronic equipment in cities, could scramble the robins' compass. When the researchers shielded the birds from that noise, the compass came back.

<break time="1.0s" />

Sea turtles might be even more impressive.

A baby turtle hatches on a beach, crawls into the ocean, and swims away. It may travel thousands of kilometers across open sea. And years later, as an adult, it comes back to the same stretch of coast where it was born, to lay its own eggs.

How? Scientists think every part of the coastline has its own magnetic signature. A slightly different strength and angle of the field. The turtle remembers the signature of home. And follows it back.

Salmon seem to do something similar, finding their way back from the ocean to the river they came from.

Now let's give you this sense.

You wake up, and before you even open your eyes, you know which way your bed is facing. North is over there. Like a gentle pull. Like knowing which way is down.

You walk outside, into a city you've never visited. No phone. No map. And you're not worried at all. You always know where north is. You always know which way home is.

Getting lost would be something that only happens to other people.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's something fascinating. There are real people who live a little like this already.

In northern Australia, there's a community that speaks a language called Guugu Yimithirr. In this language, people don't usually say "left" or "right." They use directions. North. South. East. West.

So instead of saying "the cup is on your left," they'd say something like "the cup is to your north."

To speak like this, you have to always know which way you're facing. And they do. Researchers found that speakers could point to north accurately, even inside buildings, even after traveling.

They don't have a magnetic sense. They've just trained their brains to always keep track.

<break time="1.0s" />

But your magnetic sense would show you something even bigger. The planet changing.

Earth's magnetic north pole isn't standing still. It's been drifting across the Arctic, from Canada toward Siberia. At one point, it was moving about fifty kilometers every year. Fast enough that the maps used by phones and planes need regular updates.

You'd feel north slowly sliding over the years.

And the field is getting weaker in some places. Over the South Atlantic Ocean there's a weak spot, where satellites passing overhead sometimes glitch.

Even stranger, Earth's magnetic field has completely flipped many times in the past. North became south. South became north. The last time was around seven hundred and eighty thousand years ago. Nobody knows exactly when it'll happen again.

Then there's the Sun.

Sometimes the Sun blasts out huge clouds of charged particles. When they slam into Earth's magnetic field, the field shakes. That's called a geomagnetic storm. It's what causes the northern lights.

With your new sense, a solar storm would feel like the ground shifting under your compass. You'd feel the northern lights coming, even on a cloudy night.

So, can humans really sense magnetism?

Here's the twist. Maybe. Just a little.

In twenty nineteen, scientists at Caltech put volunteers in a dark, shielded room and slowly rotated a magnetic field around them, about as strong as Earth's. The volunteers didn't notice anything. But when the scientists measured their brainwaves, some people's brains reacted to certain changes in the field.

That doesn't prove we can use it. Nobody is navigating with it. But it suggests that somewhere deep in the brain, a few of us might still be listening to the planet.

And one last warning. If you had a magnetic sense, stay away from MRI machines. An MRI scanner uses a magnet around sixty thousand times stronger than Earth's field. To you, it would feel like the whole world spinning at once.
