Glint has four minutes. Maybe less.

His grandmother is lying on the museum floor, under the great brass pendulum, and she is not breathing normally. A crowd is forming. Nobody is moving.

Glint knows exactly what to do. He has known for ten years.

But right now, his arms are the arms of a ten-year-old boy. Too light to save her.

Let's go back.

Glint is nineteen. He works the night shift at the city science museum. Tonight, a storm is rattling the glass roof, and in the main hall the Foucault pendulum swings the way it always does. A heavy brass ball on a long wire, slowly proving that the Earth is turning underneath it.

Then lightning hits the roof.

The lights die. And the pendulum stops. Mid-swing. Frozen in the air at an angle that should be impossible.

Glint, being Glint, reaches out and touches it.

He wakes up in a bed that is too small. Under a duvet covered in rockets. His feet don't reach the end of the mattress. The calendar on the wall is ten years old.

And from downstairs, he hears someone singing. Badly. In the kitchen.

Nan.

<break time="1.0s" />

So here are the rules.

In this world, the jump only goes one way, and nobody knows if it lasts. But everything else is real science.

Rule one. A ten-year-old brain is built to learn. It is more plastic, which means it rewires itself more easily. Research on language learners suggests that if you want to sound like a native speaker, starting before about age ten gives you the best chance.

Rule two. A ten-year-old body is not a grown-up body. Smaller. Lighter. Weaker. It needs nine to twelve hours of sleep a night. And almost nobody listens to it.

Rule three. Starting early is a superpower all by itself. It's called compounding.

Rule four. Change one small thing, and you might change everything. Scientists call it the butterfly effect.

Glint has a nineteen-year-old's memories. And one more thing. In his old life, when he was ten, Nan's heart failed. Help came too late. She survived, but her heart never fully recovered.

This time, he is going to stop it.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Nan is ten years younger.

She dances while she cooks. She takes the stairs two at a time, just to show off. She beats him at cards and laughs so hard she snorts. Glint hasn't heard that laugh in years. He sits at the kitchen table and just listens to it.

Plus: his brain is a sponge.

He borrows a Spanish course from the library, the one he gave up on at seventeen. In his old life, it took him months to learn a hundred words. Now, he learns a hundred a week, and his accent is better than the woman on the recording. A young brain is still pruning and strengthening its connections, and the ones you use get stronger fast. Kai thinks he's showing off. He is.

Minus: nobody takes a ten-year-old seriously.

Glint begs Nan to see a heart doctor. Nan laughs and ruffles his hair. Nan says, "I'm as fit as a fiddle, love." He tries to book the appointment himself. The receptionist asks to speak to a grown-up. And every night at nine, his small body simply switches off, mid-plan, face down on his homework.

Plus: he plays the long game.

He puts every bit of birthday money into a savings account. Here's why. At around seven percent growth a year, money roughly doubles every ten years. Start at ten instead of twenty, and you get a whole extra doubling, for free. And it doesn't just work on money. He starts walking with Nan every morning. Ten minutes. Then twenty. Regular exercise strengthens the heart. Small things, started early, add up.

Minus: the future starts to melt.

In his old life, Kai jumped off a garage roof holding an umbrella and broke his arm. This time, Glint stops him. Good. But then Kai joins football club instead of spending the summer in plaster. The team wins a match it lost before. A new kid moves in next door. Nothing matches Glint's memory anymore.

In nineteen sixty-one, a scientist named Edward Lorenz rounded one number in a weather simulation, from six decimal places to three. The forecast came out completely different. Tiny change. Totally new future.

Minus: the date is wrong.

The day Nan's heart failed in his old life was the fourteenth of March. In the kitchen. Glint has dreaded it for months. On the fourteenth, he stays home from school, watching her every second.

Nothing happens.

Nan bakes a lemon cake. Mr. Pickles steals the butter. It's a perfect, boring day. Glint cries with relief.

But the butterfly effect works both ways. He hasn't cancelled the danger.

He's just lost the date.

<break time="1.0s" />

Five weeks later, it's his birthday. Nan takes him and Kai to the science museum. His museum. Ten years before he works there.

They're standing under the pendulum when Nan goes quiet. She puts a hand on the railing. Then she folds onto the floor.

Glint is on his knees beside her in a second. She isn't responding. Her breathing is wrong. Gasping. Not normal.

This is cardiac arrest. Her heart isn't pumping, it's just quivering.

And here is the number that matters. For every minute without chest compressions and a defibrillator, the chance of surviving a cardiac arrest drops by about seven to ten percent.

Every minute counts.

He starts compressions himself. Hands in the centre of her chest. Arms straight. But on an adult, you need to push down about five to six centimetres, and his ten-year-old body is too light. He can feel it. It's not enough.

A crowd gathers. Twenty people. Thirty. Phones out. Everyone looking at each other. Nobody stepping forward.

Three minutes left. Maybe less.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint doesn't panic. Because he knows the science of crowds too.

It's called the bystander effect. In a crowd, everyone waits for someone else to act. The fix is simple. Don't ask everyone. Pick one person and give them one job.

He points at a tall man in a red coat. "You. Kneel here. Push hard and fast." The man answers in Spanish. He doesn't understand.

Glint switches to Spanish without missing a beat. Hands here. Hard. Fast. A hundred to a hundred and twenty times a minute. The man kneels and pushes.

Glint points at Kai. "Call an ambulance. Speaker on. Shout if they ask anything." Kai is very, very good at shouting.

Then Glint runs. Not to the front desk. Through the gift shop, past the dinosaur mugs, to a grey door that only staff use. Because ten years from now, he will walk past it every night. And behind it, on the wall, is a defibrillator.

He's back in ninety seconds. He presses the button. The machine talks him through every step. It will only shock if the heart needs it.

Stand clear.

Shock.

Nan takes a breath.

<break time="1.0s" />

The ambulance arrives. Nan is talking by the time they lift her. Grumbling, actually. She wants to know who's going to finish the cake.

That night, the storm comes back. Glint falls asleep in the hospital chair.

He wakes up nineteen. On the museum floor. The pendulum is swinging again.

His phone buzzes. A message from Nan. "Bring milk. Baking."

Her heart still isn't perfect. But ten years of morning walks have compound interest too.

And next to the staff door, there's a big green sign he's never seen before. Defibrillator here. Underneath it, a small brass plaque. For the ten-year-old boy who knew where it was.

Behind him, Dr. Osei is smiling. He says, "Nine years I've waited to ask. How did you know where it was?"

So that's why Dr. Osei hired him.
