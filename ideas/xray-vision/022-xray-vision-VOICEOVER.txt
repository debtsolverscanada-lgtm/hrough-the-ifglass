Kai is somewhere under forty tonnes of concrete.

The back wall of the old Regal Cinema is cracking, and the engineers say it will come down in eighteen minutes.

Glint can find him. All he has to do is take off his glasses.

But every second Glint looks, he'll be shining radiation straight at his best friend.

Let's go back. Two weeks.

It's midnight in the museum's medicine gallery. Glint is dusting a glass X-ray tube from eighteen ninety-six, one of the first ever made, built the year after Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays.

Outside, a storm. A power surge rattles the cases. The old tube flickers with a ghostly green glow, and flashes, right into Glint's eyes.

Next morning, he walks into Nan's kitchen. A skeleton is holding the teapot.

Glint screams.

The skeleton screams.

Under the table, a tiny cat skeleton hisses. Mr. Pickles, unimpressed as ever.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's the thing most films get wrong.

Real eyes can't see X-rays. They only catch visible light. And nothing about your eyes gives off X-rays either. To take an X-ray picture, a machine fires X-rays through your body onto a detector. Bones contain lots of calcium and block more of the beam, so they cast bright shadows. Metal blocks even more. Soft parts, like skin and muscle, let most of it through, so they look faint. That's why one of Röntgen's very first X-ray pictures, of his wife Anna's hand, shows thin bones, faint flesh, and a big, bold wedding ring.

So in this world, the rule is: Glint's eyes do both jobs. They shine out a faint beam of X-rays, and they catch what scatters back. A little like the body scanners some airports used to use.

Which means Glint isn't just looking.

He's shining.

And X-rays are ionising radiation. They carry enough energy to damage the DNA inside living cells. One X-ray is a tiny risk. That's why the dentist steps out of the room. But it adds up.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Nan's wedding ring.

It's been missing for three days. Nan has turned the house upside down, and she's quietly heartbroken. Glint glances at the bread rising by the oven. Inside the dough, a bright circle glows like a tiny moon. Metal blocks X-rays even better than bone. Nan cries, laughs, and then makes him eat the slice it was in.

Minus: the badge.

Dr. Osei clips a radiation badge to Nan's apron for a week. Hospital staff wear these to measure the dose they get. The result comes back. Every morning at breakfast, Glint has been looking at Nan.

Not dangerous yet. About the same as a few chest X-rays. And one chest X-ray is about the same as ten days of the natural background radiation we all get from rocks, food and space.

But Nan has a weak heart, and Glint feels sick.

So Dr. Osei gives him new glasses. Round. Gold. Lenses made of lead glass. Lead glass lets light through but blocks X-rays. It's what the windows in hospital X-ray rooms are made of. Glasses on, normal eyes. Glasses off, the power is on.

Plus: the museum.

In the fossil store, there's a block of rock nobody has opened in eighty years. Late at night, glasses off, Glint sees inside it. A tiny skull. Museums really do use X-rays and scanners to look inside fossils and mummies without breaking them. Dr. Osei calls it a lucky guess.

Minus: everyone's a skeleton.

At Kai's birthday party, Glint slips his glasses off for one second. Twenty grinning skulls. No faces, no smiles, no idea who's who. Kai thinks it's hilarious. Kai says, "Tell me I've got a great skull."

He does have a great skull.

Plus: Kai's arm.

When he was fourteen, Kai rode a shopping trolley down the canal steps. Terrible idea. Broken arm. Now there's a titanium plate in there, with six screws. Kai begs for one look. A bright stripe, six bright dots, like a tiny constellation. Kai says it's the coolest thing about him.

Minus: the beam has no off switch.

Dr. Osei is serious now. The X-ray body scanners some airports used gave a dose so small it's like a minute or two of flying. Glint's eyes are much stronger than that.

Radiographers learn three rules. Time: look for as short as possible. Distance: move away from the source, because radiation spreads out. Double the distance, and the dose drops to a quarter. And shielding: put something in the way.

Glint promises. Glasses on. Always.

He keeps that promise for four days.

<break time="1.0s" />

Friday night. Storm. And Kai has had another terrible idea.

He's filming a ghost-hunting video inside the Regal Cinema, which has been closed for years. Halfway through, the roof gives way.

When Glint arrives, the street is blue lights and rain. Firefighters are shouting Kai's name into the dust. No answer. Their thermal camera can't see through concrete. The search dog is twenty minutes away.

And the back wall is leaning. The engineer gives it eighteen minutes. After that, nobody goes in.

Nan is at the barrier in her coat and slippers, one hand pressed to her chest. Kai has eaten at her table every Sunday since he was six. Nan says, "Glint. Do something."

Glint's hand goes to his glasses.

But a long stare means a big dose, straight into Kai. And searching a whole cinema's worth of rubble could take minutes.

Fifteen minutes.

He thinks about Dr. Osei's three rules.

Time. Distance. Shielding.

Fourteen.

Somewhere under there, Kai might be hurt, cold, and running out of air. Every minute Glint stands here deciding is a minute Kai doesn't have.

He looks up at the fire engine's ladder.

<break time="1.0s" />

Distance first. Glint begs a firefighter to take him up the ladder, thirty metres above the rubble. Every time he doubles his distance, the dose to Kai drops to a quarter.

Shielding is already there. The concrete itself soaks up most of the beam before it can reach anyone underneath.

And time. He'll allow himself one look. One second.

But what can he find in one second? The rubble is full of steel bars, a glowing grid of straight lines.

Then he remembers. Kai's arm.

Glasses off. One second.

Straight lines everywhere. And in the middle of them, something different. A short bright stripe with six bright dots.

Glasses on.

The firefighter beside him frowns. A museum assistant, pointing at rubble? But there's no better plan, and the clock is running.

Glint shouts down. Left side, under the old projection stairs, three metres in. And don't touch the big slab. In that same second, he saw it hanging by just two steel bars.

Nine minutes.

The firefighters dig. Six. Three. A dusty hand pushes up through the rubble, still holding a phone.

Kai croaks, "Did you get that on camera?"

They carry him out. Forty seconds later, the back wall crashes down.

<break time="1.0s" />

Dr. Osei checks one last badge. Glint's own. Clipped to his collar, right next to his eyes.

The number is the highest of all.

Because the beam starts at Glint's eyes, and the lens of the eye is one of the most radiation-sensitive parts of the body. Too much can cause cataracts, a clouding that blurs your sight. Every look he gave the world, his own eyes took the biggest dose.

Everyone worried about who Glint was looking at.

Nobody thought about who was doing the looking.

Glint puts the lead glasses on. This time, for good.

On Sunday, Nan hands him a small box. Inside, a brand-new pair of round gold frames, lead glass lenses, and engraved on the arm, three words. Time. Distance. Shielding.

Nan says, "You never needed X-ray eyes to see what matters, love."

The power is still there. Behind the glass.

Waiting.
