Kai has forty minutes.

He's somewhere in Harrow Woods, at the bottom of a hole nobody can see, with a twisted ankle and a dead phone. The sun sets in forty minutes. Snow is coming. After dark, the rescue team has to stop searching until morning.

Glint stands at the edge of a thousand acres of trees.

He isn't listening for people.

He's listening to the crows.

<break time="1.0s" />

Let's go back. Ten days.

The city science museum is opening a new exhibit called Wild Voices. A giant sound dome, playing animal calls from all over the planet. Whale clicks. Bee buzz. Birdsong.

It's two in the morning, and Glint is testing the speakers, headphones on, when a power surge rips through the building. Every speaker screams at once.

He wakes up on the floor. His headphones are smoking.

On the window ledge, a pigeon tilts its head. And Glint hears, as clearly as a radio, "Warm. Warm. Crumbs?"

He stares at the pigeon.

The pigeon stares back.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules. And they matter.

In this world, Glint doesn't hear animals speaking English. He decodes what animals actually communicate. Nothing more.

And real science says animals communicate far more than we used to think.

Take prairie dogs. For decades, biologist Con Slobodchikoff recorded their alarm calls in Arizona. He found different calls for hawks, coyotes, dogs and humans. And when people walked through the colony wearing different coloured shirts, the calls changed. Prairie dogs were describing what kind of predator was coming, roughly how big it was, and even what colour it was wearing.

So the power is real information. Danger. Food. Family. Who's who.

But it's not poetry. A pigeon will never tell Glint the meaning of life. Mostly, a pigeon wants crumbs.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Mr. Pickles, finally explained.

Here's a real fact. Adult cats hardly ever meow at each other. Meowing is mostly something cats do for humans. So every meow Mr. Pickles has made in fourteen years was aimed at Nan.

Glint translates for her. Food. Door. Wrong food. Door again. Why is the door like this.

Nan says, "Fourteen years, and that's all he's got to say?"

Mr. Pickles says, "Food."

Plus: the zoo.

Kai drags Glint to the prairie dog enclosure. Kai is wearing his banana-yellow hoodie, as always. A prairie dog pops up, sees him, and calls. Glint translates. Tall human. Yellow. Coming fast.

Kai is overjoyed. He has a name in prairie dog.

Minus: the crows hate Kai.

Last spring, Kai found a baby crow on the pavement and climbed a tree to put it back in its nest. Terrible idea. Young crows often hop around on the ground while learning to fly, and their parents are watching the whole time.

The parents saw Kai's face. And crows remember faces. At the University of Washington, John Marzluff's team caught crows while wearing a scary mask. For years afterwards, crows scolded anyone wearing that mask. Even crows that were never caught, because crows teach each other who's dangerous.

Now, every crow in the city screams when Kai walks by. Glint translates. It is not polite.

Plus: the bees.

At Nan's allotment, a honeybee comes home and dances. Scientists have known since Karl von Frisch won a Nobel Prize that this waggle dance is a map. The angle of the waggle shows the direction of the food, compared to the sun. The longer the waggle, the farther away it is. Glint follows the dance and finds a secret thicket of blackberries. Nan makes a crumble the size of a car tyre.

Plus: the whales.

The museum gets a live sound feed from scientists studying sperm whales in the Caribbean. Sperm whales talk in patterns of clicks called codas. Different whale clans have different dialects, like accents. Glint listens all night. In his ears, it's mostly whales checking on each other. Here. Here. Family, here.

Minus: the noise.

There's no off switch. Every pigeon, every sparrow, every dog in every garden, all talking at once, all night. Glint can't sleep. He buys noise-cancelling headphones and wears them everywhere.

Which is why, on Saturday afternoon, he doesn't hear his phone ring.

<break time="1.0s" />

At ten to five, Glint finally checks his voicemail.

It's Kai. Wind in the background. Kai says, "Going to make peace with the crows. Harrow Woods, by the old quarry. Back by four."

Then a crack. A yell. Silence.

By the time Glint reaches the woods, the mountain rescue team is already there. Their drone can't see through the pine trees. Their search dog is out on another call. The light is going. Snowflakes start to fall.

The team leader checks her watch. Forty minutes until dark. After that, the search stops until morning. And a night outside with a twisted ankle, in snow, is a night nobody wants to test.

Glint's phone buzzes. It's Nan, flour on her hands, oven on, baking because she can't do anything else. Nan says, "Bring him home, love."

Glint takes off his headphones.

The forest hits him like a wave. Robins arguing. Squirrels scolding. A wren shouting about a cat. Owls just waking up. A thousand voices, all at once.

Thirty-five minutes.

He can't find one voice in all of that. He doesn't even know which way to walk. He shouts Kai's name. The snow swallows it. No answer. Just a thousand animals who have no idea who Kai is.

Then he remembers. The crows.

Every crow in this city has learned Kai's face. Every crow hates it. And when crows spot a face they hate, they don't keep quiet. They gather, and they scream about it. Scientists call it mobbing. One crow calls, others fly in, and soon the whole gang is shouting at the same spot.

If Kai is in these woods, somewhere out there, the crows are yelling.

Glint closes his eyes. He pushes past the robins, past the wren, past the owls.

And there. Far off. Faint.

Danger face. Danger face. Down. Down.

Twenty-five minutes.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint runs.

He follows the scolding like a trail of shouting arrows. When the calls get louder and more frantic, he's getting closer. When they fade, he's going the wrong way. The crows are passing the alarm from tree to tree, and every new crow that arrives makes the signal stronger.

The rescue team runs behind him, confused, following a museum assistant who is following birds.

Fifteen minutes. The snow thickens.

Glint slips on a frozen root, scrambles up, keeps going. His lungs burn. Somewhere in the dark, a crow swoops low over his head, calling the others in.

Ten minutes.

Now the noise is right overhead. Thirty crows packed into one dead pine, all screaming down into a gully choked with brambles.

At the bottom, half buried in snow, there's a flash of banana yellow.

Kai. Freezing. Pale. But awake.

He squints up at the circling birds and croaks, "Are they still yelling at me?"

Glint says, "They found you."

The team lifts Kai out on a stretcher just as the last of the light disappears.

<break time="1.0s" />

In the back of the ambulance, Kai opens his fist. A crushed bag of peanuts.

He wasn't out there being reckless. He went to say sorry.

Glint steps outside. The crows are lined up along the car park fence, watching the ambulance. He listens.

The call has changed.

It's not danger face anymore.

Now, they're calling him peanut face.

That night, the power starts to fade. Voices soften back into chirps and barks. The last thing Glint understands is Mr. Pickles, curled on Nan's lap. The old cat looks at him and slowly blinks. Scientists say a slow blink is how cats show they're friendly.

Mr. Pickles says, "Don't tell anyone."
