What if you could hibernate like a bear?

You curl up in November. You close your eyes. And you wake up in spring. No food. No water. No bathroom breaks. Five months, gone.

It sounds like the ultimate way to skip winter. But by the end of this video, you'll find out it might be how humans finally get to Mars.

First, let's look at what a bear actually does.

When winter comes, a black bear crawls into its den and settles in. And for months, it doesn't eat. It doesn't drink. It doesn't pee. It doesn't poop.

Its heart slows down from around forty or fifty beats a minute to sometimes fewer than ten. Its breathing slows to just a few breaths a minute. Its body temperature drops a few degrees. It lives off the fat it stored up in the fall.

That alone is amazing. But here's the part that really fascinates scientists.

<break time="1.0s" />

If you stayed in bed for five months, your body would fall apart.

Your muscles would shrink. Your bones would get weaker. You'd struggle to walk when you got up. Astronauts face the same problem in space. Without gravity, they can lose around one percent of their bone mass every month.

But bears wake up in spring, stand up, and walk out of the den. Their muscles and bones are in surprisingly good shape.

How? Bears seem to recycle their own waste. Instead of getting rid of it, their bodies turn some of it back into the building blocks of protein. And something in their bodies seems to protect their bones while they're lying still.

If doctors could copy that, it could help people stuck in hospital beds. Older people losing muscle. And astronauts.

Now let's give you this power.

It's the end of October. You start eating. A lot. Bears can gain a huge amount of weight before winter, so you're doing the same. Pizza for breakfast. Cake for lunch. Your doctor says it's fine. It's for science.

Then the first snow falls. You feel incredibly sleepy. You close the curtains, climb into bed, and drift away.

Your heart slows. Your breathing slows. Your body cools down a little. And winter passes without you.

<break time="1.0s" />

You wake up in April.

You're much lighter. And your body feels surprisingly okay.

But you've missed a lot. Five months of messages. Your phone has hundreds of notifications. Your friends went to parties. There's a new song everyone is singing. Prices went up. You missed the holidays completely.

You missed the cold, dark winter. But you also missed a whole chapter of everyone else's life.

So, could humans ever really do this?

Here's something surprising. One of our relatives does.

In Madagascar, there's a small primate called the fat-tailed dwarf lemur. It's a primate, like us. And it hibernates for up to seven months a year, inside tree holes. It stores fat in its tail, and lives off it.

If a primate can hibernate, maybe the ability isn't as far away from us as we think.

<break time="1.0s" />

And doctors already use a little bit of this idea.

After some heart attacks, hospitals cool patients' bodies down a few degrees on purpose. A cooler body needs less oxygen. That can help protect the brain while the patient recovers.

And in twenty twenty, scientists found a group of brain cells in mice that, when switched on, put the mice into a hibernation-like state. Their temperature and metabolism dropped for more than a day. Then they woke up, apparently fine.

Mice don't naturally hibernate. So the switch was already there. Hidden.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now, here's why space agencies are paying attention.

A trip to Mars could take around six to nine months. Each way. That's a lot of food, water and oxygen to carry. A lot of space for the crew to move around in. A lot of time for people to get bored, stressed or sick.

But what if the crew slept through most of it?

Researchers working with NASA and the European Space Agency have studied this idea. Sleeping astronauts would need less food, less oxygen, and much smaller living spaces. The spaceship could be lighter and cheaper. And the crew would wake up near Mars, like it was the next morning.

It sounds like a science fiction movie. But it's being studied right now.

<break time="1.0s" />

There's one last strange fact.

Hibernation isn't really sleep. It's something different. And some hibernating animals, like ground squirrels, keep waking up and warming their bodies every week or so during winter. Some scientists think one reason might be that they need to warm up to get some proper sleep.

So imagine hibernating for five months... and still waking up tired.
