Eleven minutes until high tide.

The river has already burst its banks. Water is pouring down the steps of the community centre, into the basement. And in that basement, behind a door that will not open, is Nan.

The water is at her chest.

Glint can lift a bus with one finger. He can make a river stand up on its end.

But he cannot lift enough of it. Not in eleven minutes.

Let's go back.

The city science museum has one object that's older than the Earth itself. An iron meteorite, about the size of a microwave, formed in the early solar system over four and a half billion years ago.

Glint is polishing its glass case on the night shift when he notices it's humming. A low hum. Like a fridge, but deeper. He opens the case to check.

The moment his fingers touch the iron, his cup of tea lifts off the table.

So does the table.

So does Glint.

He drifts up to the ceiling, bumps gently against a hanging model of Saturn, and realises he has no idea how to get down.

Then he thinks the word "down." Hard.

And he hits the floor.

<break time="1.0s" />

So here are the rules.

On Earth, gravity pulls everything down with an acceleration of about nine point eight metres per second, every second. Drop a ball, and after one second it's falling at almost ten metres per second. After two seconds, almost twenty. Gravity doesn't just pull. It keeps speeding things up.

In this world, Glint controls that number. Inside a bubble about fifty metres wide, he can turn gravity up, down, off, or point it sideways.

Real gravity follows strict rules, and so does his. Every object inside his bubble obeys it. Water. Air. Light. Him.

And the harder he pushes, the more it costs his body.

Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces. A fridge magnet beats the entire planet every time it holds up a drawing. But gravity never stops, and it reaches across the whole universe.

Now Glint holds the dial.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Nan's shopping weighs nothing.

He carries twelve bags up the hill with one hand. Then he turns gravity down in the kitchen, just for fun. Nan floats past the fridge, laughing so hard she hiccups. Mr. Pickles drifts by upside down, looking absolutely furious.

Plus: Kai has a terrible idea.

Kai wants to throw a tennis ball into space. Never coming back. Dr. Osei does the maths. To escape Earth's gravity completely, the ball would need about eleven kilometres per second. That's more than thirty times the speed of sound. The air in front of it would heat up so much the ball would burn to a crisp in a second. Glint gives it a gentle push instead. The ball sails up, comes back down, and bonks Kai on the head.

Plus: he learns how to stay up.

Glint rises above the clouds and asks Dr. Osei a question. Why do astronauts float? Here's the surprise. At the height of the International Space Station, about four hundred kilometres up, gravity is still about ninety percent as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they're falling. The station travels sideways at around twenty-eight thousand kilometres per hour, so as it falls, the Earth curves away beneath it. It falls around the planet, forever. That's an orbit.

Minus: his bones are weakening.

Glint loves floating so much that he starts sleeping weightless. After three weeks, his wrist aches. Dr. Osei scans it. In space, without gravity pulling on them, astronauts can lose about one to one and a half percent of the bone mass in their hips and spine every month. Bones need weight to stay strong. Glint has been taking it away.

Minus: the city sees him.

One night, Glint pushes gravity hard, just to see what happens. The streetlights around him smear into glowing arcs. Gravity bends light. Einstein predicted it. In nineteen-nineteen, astronomers watching a solar eclipse measured starlight bending around the Sun. Normally, you'd need a star-sized mass to notice it. Glint makes it happen on a street corner. Somebody films it. By morning, it has three million views.

Minus: the biggest tide of the year is coming.

The Moon's gravity pulls on the oceans, raising the water into bulges that sweep around the planet. That's why most coasts get two high tides a day. When the Sun and Moon line up, their pulls combine into extra-high spring tides. This Saturday brings a spring tide, and a storm pushing seawater up the river.

Nan is at the community centre, baking for the flood volunteers.

Because Nan bakes when she's worried.

<break time="1.0s" />

The storm hits at nine.

By half past, the river is over the wall. Kai calls, shouting over the rain. Mr. Pickles bolted into the community centre basement, and Nan went down after him. Then the water came in through the basement windows. Now the door at the bottom of the stairs won't open.

Glint flies there in under a minute. Over rooftops. Through the rain. Below him, cars are floating down streets that were dry an hour ago.

He tries the obvious thing. He points gravity upward over the river and pulls.

The water rises. A huge, shining hump of river lifts into the air, like the Moon's tide squeezed into one street. The light bends around Glint into a glowing ring. Half the city sees it.

But water is heavy. One cubic metre weighs a tonne. The river is millions of tonnes, and more pours in from the sea every second.

He pulls harder. The ring of light tightens. Windows rattle across the whole riverbank.

His wrist cracks. Three weeks of floating have left his bones too weak for this. He gasps and lets go, and the river slams back down with a roar that shakes the street.

Kai is hammering on the basement door. It won't budge. Nan's voice comes through, faint.

Nan says, "Don't you dare do anything daft, love."

Eight minutes until high tide. The water is rising toward her shoulders.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint stops. He stops trying to be strong. And thinks like a scientist instead.

Why won't the door open?

Because the water on the other side is pressing against it. And water pressure depends on three things. How dense the water is. How deep it is. And how strong gravity is.

Glint can't change the first two.

But he controls the third.

He doesn't need to move the river. He only needs to make the water in one basement weigh nothing.

He kneels at the top of the stairs. Focuses on the stairwell and the basement. And turns gravity off.

Zero.

The water stops pushing. Kai shoves, and the door swings open like it's made of paper.

Inside, the flood has broken into floating blobs, wobbling and glittering like giant jellies. And in the middle, drifting gently, is Nan. Holding a soaking wet, very angry cat.

Kai swims through the air and pulls them out.

Then Glint brings gravity back slowly. A little at a time. Because the moment he lets go, everything in that room will start falling at nine point eight metres per second, every second.

<break time="1.0s" />

The next morning, the meteorite has stopped humming. Glint's power is gone.

His wrist is in a cast. Nan has already signed it.

Then Dr. Osei shows him an email. It's from an observatory on the other side of the world.

Last night, starlight over your city bent for eleven seconds. No known object could do that. What happened there?

And at the bottom, one more line.

We recorded the same effect three weeks ago. In a different city.

Glint isn't the only one.
