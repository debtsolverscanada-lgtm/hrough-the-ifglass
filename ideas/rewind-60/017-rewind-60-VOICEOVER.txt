Kai is hanging from the hands of a clock.

Forty metres above the city. Fingers wrapped around the iron minute hand of the museum's clock tower. Every sixty seconds, the hand jumps one notch lower. Every notch, it tilts a little steeper. Soon, Kai will slide right off the end.

Below him, the street lights look like grains of sugar. Kai is not afraid of anything. He is afraid now.

Glint can rewind time.

But only sixty seconds. And he has one rewind left.

<break time="1.0s" />

Let's go back. One week.

The museum's oldest treasure is its tower clock. It's a hundred and fifty years old, with a brass pendulum taller than a man, and Glint's job tonight is to oil the gears. The tower creaks. A storm grumbles in the distance. He reaches past the pendulum for the oil can, and his watch catches on a brass wheel.

Lightning cracks overhead.

Every gear in the room spins backwards.

Then everything is still. The oil can is back on its shelf. The clock reads one minute earlier. And Glint remembers a minute that never happened.

He tries it again. Snap his fingers, think "back." The rain un-falls. Sixty seconds rewind.

Every time.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules.

In this world, Glint can send the whole universe back sixty seconds. Not a second more. And he can't stack them. Rewind twice, and he only lands sixty seconds before now. Never further.

Only one thing comes back with him. His memory. But real science puts a strict limit on that.

New memories don't lock in instantly. Your brain needs hours, and usually sleep, to store them properly. So in this world, Glint only keeps what he's actively holding in mind at the moment he rewinds. And the part of your brain that holds things in mind, your working memory, is tiny. Scientists estimate it holds about four chunks of information at once.

Four things. That's all he can carry back.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Nan's plates.

Nan drops her best plate. Crash. Glint rewinds. This time, he catches it.

Here's why that's amazing. An average person needs about a quarter of a second to react to something they see. Try catching a ruler someone drops between your fingers with no warning. It slips past before your hand even closes. But Glint knows exactly when it will fall. And knowing the future beats any reaction time.

Nan says, "How did you do that?" He tells her it's the scarf.

Minus: The lottery.

Kai's terrible idea. Watch the lottery draw, rewind, buy the winning ticket. Glint tries it. He rewinds. And the balls come out completely different.

That's chaos. Real science. In nineteen sixty-one, a weather scientist named Edward Lorenz reran a computer forecast, but typed one number slightly rounded off. A tiny difference, in the fourth decimal place. The forecast came out wildly different. He later asked whether a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas.

A spinning lottery machine is chaos too. And Glint's every breath, every step, nudges the air a little differently each time. So the balls never fall the same way twice.

Dr. Osei shows him the proof in the museum's own physics hall. A single pendulum, swinging. Start it from the same spot, and it does the same thing every time. Then a double pendulum, one arm hinged onto another. Start it from what looks like the same spot, and within seconds it's flailing in a completely new pattern.

Dr. Osei says, "One pendulum, you can predict forever. Two, and you can't predict the next ten seconds."

Plus: Perfect words.

Nan is sad on the anniversary of Grandad's death. Glint says the wrong thing. Rewind. Says something better. Rewind. On the fourth try, he says exactly the right thing, and she laughs for the first time all day.

Minus: The forgetting.

Dr. Osei tests him with a list of twenty words, then has him rewind. He brings back four. Maybe five. Every rewind, he has to choose what to keep, and everything else is gone for good.

Minus: The ghosts.

By day five, Glint's head aches. He remembers dozens of minutes that never happened. Conversations nobody else was in. A dropped plate nobody else saw break.

Dr. Osei looks at his scans and goes very quiet. Dr. Osei says, "Your brain is carrying memories with no place to file them. Stop, Glint. A few more rewinds, at most."

Glint promises to stop.

And that's when Kai gets his most terrible idea yet.

<break time="1.0s" />

It's Nan's birthday. Kai has painted a giant banner. "Happy Birthday Nan." His plan is to hang it from the museum clock face, so she'll see it from her kitchen window.

At midnight, he climbs out through the maintenance hatch.

At twelve fourteen, Glint hears him yell.

Kai's foot slipped on the wet stone. Now he's hanging off the end of the minute hand, just past the three. Behind the clock face, Glint hears the gears clunk. The hand jumps one notch. Kai slides a few centimetres toward the tip.

In this old tower, the minute hand jumps once a minute. And every jump tips it further down.

Glint shouts that he's coming. Kai shouts that he'd rather Glint hurried.

First try. Glint grabs a coil of rope and throws it out of the hatch. The wind catches it. It misses by a metre. Kai's fingers slip. Rewind.

Second try. Same throw. Same spot. But the wind gusts differently. Chaos, again. The rope misses by two metres. Rewind.

Third try. Glint lies flat and reaches. Kai lets go and grabs for his hand. A quarter of a second. That's how long Glint's brain takes to react. In that time, Kai drops about thirty centimetres. Their fingers brush. And miss.

Rewind.

Glint is gasping. The world is grey at the edges. One more, and that's it.

And Kai has three notches left.

Across the city, a kitchen light is on. Nan is up late, icing her own birthday cake. She has no idea.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint stops. Thinks.

He can't beat the wind. Wind is chaos. He can't beat his own reaction time. That's biology.

But one thing in this tower is never chaotic. The pendulum.

A simple pendulum is one of the most predictable things in physics. That's why we built clocks around them. Its every swing takes the same time. And in a tower clock like this one, the pendulum is what lets the gears move. Each swing releases the gears one tiny step.

Stop the pendulum, and the clock stops. And so does the minute hand.

He has room for four things. Not a fifth. He closes his eyes and lets go of everything else. The lottery. The plates. The three times he watched Kai fall. He chooses carefully.

Left stairs. Red door. Grab the pendulum. Then the rope.

Snap.

Sixty seconds back. The rope is coiled at his feet again. Kai's yell is still echoing. Glint runs. Left stairs, three at a time. Red door, shoulder first. The pendulum room is thick with dust and the slow, heavy tick of the swing. He throws his arms around the huge brass bob and digs in his heels. It drags him a step. Then it slows. And stops.

Outside, the minute hand freezes, mid-tilt.

Now there's no rush. Glint lowers the rope straight down from the hatch. No throwing. No wind games. Kai loops it under his arms. And Glint hauls his best friend, inch by inch, back inside.

<break time="1.0s" />

They lie on the tower floor, laughing and shaking.

Then Kai says something odd. He says, "Weird. I feel like I've fallen three times tonight."

Glint can't tell him. He'll be carrying those three falls alone. Forever.

The next morning, Nan finally sees the banner from her window. She laughs so hard she has to sit down.

Kai spelled it "Happy Brithday."

And Glint has no rewinds left.
