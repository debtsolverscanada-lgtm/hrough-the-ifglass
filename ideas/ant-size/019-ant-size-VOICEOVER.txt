Glint is two centimetres tall, standing on top of Nan's fridge.

Below him, a pan of oil is heating on the stove. It has started to smoke. Across the kitchen, Nan is fast asleep in her armchair.

Glint is screaming her name as loud as he can.

She can't hear a thing.

And that pan is getting hotter every second.

Let's go back. Two days.

The museum's new exhibit is called Tiny Giants. Ants, beetles, spiders, all blown up to the size of cars. The centrepiece is an old brass projector that makes things look huge on a giant screen.

Glint is cleaning its lens after closing. He leans into the beam to check for dust. The bulb flickers. The projector whirs backwards, and the beam goes from white to deep violet.

The room starts growing.

The ceiling soars away. The floor rushes up. The carpet becomes a forest of fibres taller than he is.

When it stops, Glint is standing next to a dust bunny the size of a sofa.

He's two centimetres tall. The size of a big ant.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules. And they all come from one piece of real science. The square-cube law.

Galileo worked it out almost four hundred years ago. When something shrinks, its surface and its cross-sections shrink by the square, and its volume and weight shrink by the cube.

Glint is now about ninety times shorter. So the cross-section of his muscles, which sets how strong they are, shrinks about eight thousand times. But his weight shrinks more than seven hundred thousand times.

That means compared to his own weight, he's about ninety times stronger. Air resistance matters more than gravity. And water behaves like glue.

His voice gets squeaky, too. Smaller vocal cords vibrate faster, and that makes a higher pitch. That's real.

And in this world, his brain still works perfectly, even though it's tiny. That part is pure fiction.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Strength.

Kai carries Glint home in a matchbox. On Nan's kitchen counter, Glint walks up to a sugar cube and lifts it over his head. To him, it's about forty times his own weight.

Real ants do this every day. Many can carry ten to fifty times their own weight. Not because they're magic. Because they're small.

Kai cheers. Glint takes a bow and drops the sugar cube on his own foot.

Minus: Water.

Glint gets thirsty and walks up to a single drop of spilled tea. He touches it. And it grabs him.

That's surface tension. The surface of water pulls together like a stretchy skin. For you, it's almost nothing. For something ant-sized, a single drop can trap you. Insects survive with water-repellent hairs. Glint has a scarf.

Kai rescues him with a corner of a paper towel. Glint drinks after that by scooping tiny beads of water, very carefully.

Plus: Falling.

Kai's terrible idea. "If you're that small, you can't get hurt falling. Right?"

Kai is right. Small things hit a much lower top speed when they fall, because air resistance is huge compared to their weight. The scientist J. B. S. Haldane wrote nearly a hundred years ago that you could drop a mouse down a deep mine shaft and it would walk away. Insects are tinier still.

Glint steps off the kitchen table. He drifts down and lands with a gentle bump. He does it four more times. It's the best fun he's had in years.

Minus: Voices.

Small animals often experience time faster than we do. Flies, for example, see more flickers per second than people can. In this world, Glint's tiny brain speeds up too. So to him, everyone talks slowly. Deeply. Nan's voice sounds like a whale singing underwater.

And his own voice is a high, thin whistle.

Kai can hear it, just. But as people get older, the very highest sounds are usually the first ones they stop hearing. Nan leans close to the matchbox and says, "Glint, love? Are you talking?"

He's shouting.

She can't hear him at all.

Minus: Everything else.

Small bodies lose heat fast, because they have so much skin for so little body. That's why tiny animals like shrews have to eat almost constantly. Glint shivers all the time, and eats crumbs all day.

And there's Mr. Pickles.

Cats can hear sounds far higher than any human can. That's partly how they hunt mice, by their squeaks. Every time Glint speaks, the grumpy old cat's ears swivel straight toward him.

Kai races to the museum, to help Dr. Osei reverse the projector. He puts Glint on top of the fridge, where the cat can't reach.

And Nan, worried sick, does what she always does when she's worried.

She bakes.

<break time="1.0s" />

Doughnuts. A deep pan of oil, heating on the stove. Nan sits down in her armchair while it warms. Just for a minute.

Her eyes close.

Glint watches the pan from the top of the fridge. The oil shimmers. Then a thin grey thread of smoke rises from it.

When oil smokes, it's getting dangerously hot. Leave it heating, and it can burst into flames. Pan fires like this are one of the most common ways kitchen fires start.

Glint yells. Nan doesn't stir. His voice is too high for her ears, and far too small.

The phone is on the counter. But his voice is too high and too faint for the phone to carry. And his finger is too tiny to work the screen. Being ninety times stronger for your size still doesn't make you strong. Not compared to a human-sized world.

The smoke thickens.

Nan's chair is four metres away. To Glint, that's like walking the length of three football pitches.

He doesn't have that kind of time. And even if he did, what then? He can't shake her awake. He weighs less than a paper clip.

The thread of smoke becomes a ribbon. Somewhere in the slow, deep hum of the house, the clock ticks like a church bell.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then Glint looks down. And sees two green eyes, staring back up at him.

Nan can't hear him. But the cat can.

Glint does the bravest thing of his life. He cups his hands and squeaks, as high and loud as he can. Like a mouse.

Mr. Pickles is on his feet instantly.

Glint jumps.

Two metres of air. To a person, that would be terrifying. But for something ant-sized, air resistance is his parachute. He drifts down and hits the lino with a soft bump. And runs.

Squeak. The cat follows. Squeak. Straight across the floor. Every padded paw step shakes the lino like an earthquake. To Glint, the cat moves in slow motion, a mountain of orange fur drifting after him. That speedy little brain is the only reason he stays ahead. Under the table. Round a chair leg. To Nan's armchair.

Glint grabs the hanging blanket and climbs. Ninety times stronger, for his size. He scrambles up the wool like a ladder, all the way into Nan's lap.

Mr. Pickles leaps after him.

Five kilograms of cat lands on Nan's knees.

Nan wakes with a shriek. And then she smells the smoke.

She does exactly the right thing. She turns off the heat. She slides a lid over the pan. And she does not move it, and she does not throw water on it. Water on hot oil can explode into a fireball.

The smoke curls away. The kitchen is safe.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then Nan looks down at her lap.

Mr. Pickles is there. And in his mouth, gently, held by the back of his scarf, the way a mother cat carries a kitten, is Glint.

Not a scratch.

The cat drops him softly into Nan's palm and starts to purr.

Mr. Pickles was never hunting him. Maybe, to an old cat, a cry that high and that small sounded like a kitten in trouble. Every time those ears swivelled toward Glint, the grumpy old cat had been listening out for him. Guarding him, all along.

That night, Kai and Dr. Osei bring Glint back to full size. And Mr. Pickles sleeps on his pillow.
