## Fact check
- Human hearing tops out around 20 kHz. Bat calls run about 20–120 kHz, and some species exceed 200 kHz.
- Some bats call at about 120–140 dB SPL at 10 cm. Middle-ear muscles contract during the call to protect hearing.
- Bats detect wires about 0.08 mm thick (Griffin's experiments).
- The feeding buzz reaches up to about 200 calls per second.
- Sound travels at 343 m/s. The echo from a wall 2 m away returns in about 0.012 s.
- Glass reflects sound. Soft materials absorb it. Sound diffracts around corners.
- Dolphin echolocation clicks partly penetrate soft tissue. Medical ultrasound works on the same principle (MHz).
- Noctuid moths hear bats and dive. The tiger moth *Bertholdia trigona* jams bat sonar with ultrasonic clicks (Corcoran et al., *Science* 2009).
- Daniel Kish lost both eyes to retinoblastoma by 13 months old and echolocates with tongue clicks (World Access for the Blind).
- Thaler et al. 2011 (*PLoS ONE*): echolocators process echoes in the visual cortex.
- Norman et al. 2021 (*PLoS ONE*, Durham University): blind and sighted volunteers learned click-based echolocation in 10 weeks.
- The hand "shhh" demo is a standard echolocation teaching exercise.
