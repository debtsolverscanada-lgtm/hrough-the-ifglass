In two hours, Glint will be stuck in his bully's body forever.

He's standing in a children's hospital at midnight. Six foot four. Fists like loaves of bread. This body belongs to Dex Harlow, the boy who called him Goggles for eight years and knocked his lunch tray flying every day of school.

A little girl is holding his hand, and she won't let go.

Across the city, Dex is wearing Glint's face.

And the only machine that can swap them back is a smoking box.

Let's go back. Seven days.

The city science museum has a new exhibit called Swap Seats. Two chairs. Two helmets with tiny cameras. Two headsets. You sit opposite a stranger, the cameras trade what you see, and for one minute you see yourself through their eyes.

It's two in the morning. Glint is testing it alone when the night guard walks in. The usual guard is off sick. The agency has sent a cover.

Dex Harlow.

Dex laughs. Dex says, "Still playing with toys, Goggles?"

He drops into the other chair, jams on the helmet, and slams his fist on the control desk to make it go faster.

A spark. A bang. Darkness.

Glint wakes up on the floor. The floor seems very far away. His hands are enormous.

Across the room, his own face is staring at him. Screaming.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules. And they're stranger than the swap.

Your brain keeps a map of your body. In the nineteen thirties, the surgeon Wilder Penfield gently touched the brains of awake patients during operations, and found a strip that maps every part of you. It's a weird map. Huge hands. Huge lips. Tiny back. Scientists draw it as a little man called the homunculus.

You also have a hidden sense called proprioception. Sensors in your muscles and joints tell your brain where every limb is, without looking.

And here's the scary part. The map can be fooled. In nineteen ninety-eight, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen hid a person's real hand and put a rubber hand on the table. They stroked both with paintbrushes at exactly the same time. Within minutes, many people felt the brush on the rubber hand. Their brains had claimed it.

In two thousand and eight, Valeria Petkovic and Henrik Ehrsson went further. With cameras on another person's head and a simple handshake, volunteers felt they were inside the other person's body, shaking hands with themselves.

In this world, the rig didn't just fool them. It moved them. And the rule is: after seven days, each brain redraws its map for good. Then the swap locks.

<break time="1.0s" />

Minus: everything is the wrong size.

Glint's brain still thinks he's five foot nine. Different height, different muscles, even different hormones. He hits his head on the bus door. The kitchen doorframe. Every doorframe. His brain plans every move with the old map, and the new body doesn't match.

Plus: strength.

Nan's jam jar, the one nobody has opened since summer. Pop. Kai's jaw drops.

But here's a real fact. Your brain sets your grip before your fingers touch anything, based on what it expects. Glint's brain expects skinny arms. He crushes three coffee cups before lunch.

Minus: he's always starving.

A bigger body with more muscle burns more energy just sitting still. Dex's body wants four breakfasts. Nan, delighted, bakes for an army.

Dr. Osei says it's the closest thing to a head transplant anyone has ever lived through. Real surgeons have tried. In nineteen seventy, Robert White moved a monkey's head onto another body. It could see and taste, but it could never move that body, because a cut spinal cord can't be reconnected. In two thousand and seventeen, a surgeon announced the first human head transplant. It never happened.

Plus: the flat.

On day two, Glint lets himself into Dex's home with Dex's keys. He expects trophies. Loud music.

Instead, there's a tiny kitchen covered in sticky notes in Dex's handwriting. Poppy, no crusts. Poppy, hospital bag. Dad, tablets at six.

Dex's dad hurt his back on a building site last year and can barely stand. And Poppy is eight, with a gap in her teeth and a big operation on her hip next Saturday.

Every night, Dex cooks, checks homework, and braids Poppy's hair. Badly.

Poppy holds Glint's hand and squeezes three times. That means I love you. Dex always squeezes back four. I love you more.

Glint squeezes back four.

Then Poppy says, "Read me the fact book." Glint has no idea what that is. Poppy says, "Dex hides it. Nobody else is allowed to see it."

Minus: Dex gets the Glint treatment.

At the bus stop, a big guy shoves him aside. At the museum, people talk right over him. Then he walks into his own gym, and his own mates laugh at him. Goggles. The word he taught them.

That night, Dex phones. In Glint's voice, small and quiet, Dex says, "Is it always like this?"

Glint says, "Since we were eleven."

A long silence. Then Dex says, "Sorry."

Minus: it's starting to fit.

By Thursday, Glint stops hitting his head. He ducks without thinking. Dex stops tripping over Glint's shorter legs. The rubber hand took minutes. Their brains have had five days.

The map is redrawing.

And Dr. Osei looks at the burnt rig and says it will take two weeks to rebuild.

<break time="1.0s" />

Friday. Eleven at night. The swap locks at two in the morning, exactly seven days after the spark.

Poppy is in the children's hospital, ready for surgery at eight. Her dad has fallen asleep in the chair. She's terrified. She won't let go of her big brother's hand.

Except it isn't her brother.

Glint can't leave. If he leaves, she goes into surgery alone and scared.

Midnight. Across the city, Dr. Osei says the rig can't be saved.

Dex calls. His voice, Glint's voice, cracks. If the swap locks, Poppy wakes up from her operation with a stranger for a brother. And Nan loses her grandson to the boy who bullied him.

One hour and fifty minutes.

Glint looks down at the tiny hand in his huge one.

And he thinks about the rubber hand.

Botvinick and Cohen didn't use a magic machine. A rubber hand and two paintbrushes. Ehrsson's body swap used two cameras, two headsets and a handshake. The trick was never in the machine. It's in the brain. See the touch. Feel the touch. At the same moment.

Glint calls Kai.

<break time="1.0s" />

Kai grabs two cardboard headsets from the museum gift shop, two old phones, and Dex, and pedals across the city like a maniac.

One fifteen. They burst into the children's ward.

The plan. Each of them straps a phone to his forehead, streaming to the other one's headset. So Glint sees through the eyes of his old body, and Dex sees through his.

Then they need a touch. The same touch, on both hands, at exactly the same moment, again and again, until each brain says, that hand is mine.

Glint looks at Poppy. Poppy holds out both hands.

One forty.

Poppy squeezes. Both hands at once. Three squeezes. Three squeezes. Three squeezes.

Through the headset, Glint watches his own thin hand being squeezed. And he feels it.

Something lurches.

He opens his eyes. He's small. The bed is very far up. His gold glasses are back on his nose.

One fifty-eight.

Opposite, Dex looks down at his own big hands. Then at Poppy. And he squeezes back four.

<break time="1.0s" />

The operation goes perfectly.

When Poppy wakes up, sleepy and grinning, she asks for the fact book. Dex goes red. He pulls a battered notebook from inside his pillowcase, covered in gold star stickers.

Glint knows that notebook.

He wrote it. When he was eleven. Every cool fact he knew. On the first day of secondary school, Dex snatched it and threw it in the bin.

Dex didn't throw it away. He's read it to Poppy every night for years.

Poppy points at Glint and whispers, "Is that the fact book man?"

Dex says, "Yeah. That's Glint."

The first time in eight years he's ever used his name.
