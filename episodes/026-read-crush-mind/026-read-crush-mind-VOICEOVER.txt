Dr. Osei is about to drive home half asleep, and only Glint knows.

He's been awake for twenty hours. In fifteen minutes, he'll be alone on the motorway, in the dark, with ninety minutes to go.

Glint knows, because he heard Dr. Osei think it.

Tonight, Glint can hear what people think. His best friend. His mentor. And the girl he's liked for a whole year.

And nobody is going to believe him.

<break time="1.0s" />

Let's go back. Three hours.

It's Late Night at the city science museum. Music, lanterns, three hundred guests, and a new exhibit called Mind Readers.

The star of the show is an old brain scanner helmet, switched on for one night only. Its sensors sit in liquid helium, at minus two hundred and sixty-nine degrees Celsius.

Glint is helping. So is Maya, the volunteer who runs the planetarium shows. Glint has liked Maya for a year. In that year, he has said eleven words to her. Three of them were "sorry" and two were "excuse me".

Kai says, "Tonight's the night. Talk to her." Glint says, "Tonight's the night I hide behind the dinosaur."

Then a pipe cracks. The helium shrieks out in a freezing white cloud. Glint and Maya are knocked flat.

Glint sits up, ears ringing. He hears Maya say, "Gold glasses. Please be okay."

But her lips didn't move.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules. And they're based on real machines.

In twenty twenty-three, scientists at the University of Texas at Austin built a decoder that turned brain scans into sentences. Each volunteer spent about sixteen hours in a scanner, listening to podcasts, so the computer could learn their brain.

It never read exact words. It read the gist. One person heard, "I don't have my driver's license yet." The decoder wrote, "She has not even started to learn to drive yet."

It only worked on the person it was trained on. And only if they cooperated. When volunteers counted backwards by sevens in their heads, the story fell apart.

Other teams, like Meta's, use scanners that catch the brain's tiny magnetic fields, thousands of times a second. The scientists themselves say mental privacy matters, and that this only works on people who agree to it.

In this world, Glint's brain has become the decoder. And like the real one, it's trained by listening. People whose voices he's heard for hours come through clear. Strangers are a mumble.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Maya.

Glint has watched every one of her Friday planetarium talks. That's a lot of hours. Kai calls it training. Glint calls it astronomy.

So her thoughts come through crystal clear. They're short, like text messages. Real scientists say inner speech is like that. Squashed. Words left out. And much faster than talking.

Maya spots him in the crowd. Her thought arrives. "Gold glasses. Here again. Good."

Glint thinks, "Say something clever about Saturn."

And Maya says, out loud, "Go on, then. Say something clever about Saturn."

He must have muttered it.

Plus: Nan.

Nan is running the cake stall. Out loud, she tells a judge, "Lovely sponge, dear." Inside her head, Nan says, "That sponge is a brick."

Here's a real finding. When researchers beeped people at random moments and asked what was in their heads, inner speech showed up in roughly a quarter of the moments. Some people talk to themselves constantly. Nan never stops.

Plus: Kai.

Kai's head is surprisingly quiet. No words. Just pictures. A hot dog. A skateboard. A bigger hot dog.

That's real too. Some people say they have little or no inner voice at all. They think in images and feelings.

Minus: the noise.

Three hundred guests. Strangers mumble like a radio in another room. His people are loud. He tries not to listen. It's like trying not to read a sign.

Glint learns a trick his brain already knows. The cocktail party effect. In a noisy room, you can tune in to one voice and let the rest blur.

But some words break through anyway. Especially your own name. In one classic experiment, about a third of people noticed their own name in a conversation they were told to ignore.

Minus: what Kai really thinks.

Across the hall, the word Glint pops out of the noise. It's Kai, talking to Nan. Kai is thinking, "He'll never ask her. He never goes for anything. He'll be stuck on night shift forever."

That one hurts.

Then Kai thinks, "Best person I know. Wish he could see it."

Glint doesn't know whether to laugh or cry.

Minus: Dr. Osei.

Glint tunes in to his mentor. Dr. Osei's thoughts are slow and heavy. "Awake since four. Airport at two. Windows down, radio loud. I'll be fine."

He won't be fine.

<break time="1.0s" />

It's eleven forty-five. Dr. Osei's daughter lands at two. The airport is ninety minutes away.

Glint knows the science. After about seventeen hours awake, people react about as badly as someone who's had too much to drink. After twenty, it's worse. And the tired brain starts taking microsleeps. Tiny naps, a few seconds long. At motorway speed, three seconds is about the length of a football pitch.

Worse, people often don't notice their own microsleeps.

Cold air and a loud radio? In driving tests, they only helped a little, and not for long.

Glint says, "You look really tired." Dr. Osei laughs. "Three coffees, Glint. I'm fine."

Glint grabs Kai. "He can't drive. I heard him think it."

Kai stares. Then Kai thinks, "Has he hit his head?"

Nan is surrounded by judges. Maya is mid-show. And what would he even tell them? I can hear thoughts now?

Twelve minutes.

Dr. Osei finds his keys. His thoughts are breaking up. "Keys. Coat. Ama. Motorway."

Then, for two seconds, nothing at all. His head dips. It snaps back up.

He didn't even notice.

Eight minutes.

Dr. Osei walks out into the car park. Glint runs after him.

Five minutes.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then Glint remembers the rules. Dr. Osei's own rules.

He catches up at the car. "You told us how people beat the decoder. Counting backwards by sevens. So try it. Count in your head, starting at one hundred. If I can tell you exactly what you counted, you give me your keys."

Dr. Osei smiles. He's a scientist. He can't resist a test.

Here's the clever part. In the real study, counting didn't switch the brain off. It just gave it something else to think about, so the decoder lost the story.

Glint doesn't want the story. He wants the numbers.

Dr. Osei closes his eyes.

Glint listens. "One hundred. Ninety-three. Eighty-six. Eighty. No. Seventy-nine. Seventy... three?"

Glint says it all back. Every number. Every mistake.

Dr. Osei goes very still. That's proof of the impossible.

And proof of something worse. The man who taught Glint maths just failed to subtract seven.

He hands over the keys.

A taxi takes him to the airport. He sleeps the whole way. At half past two, Glint's phone buzzes. "Ama is safe. Thank you. We need to talk."

<break time="1.0s" />

One in the morning. The planetarium is empty, except for Maya.

Glint sits beside her. He thinks, "Say something. Tell her she's brighter than every star in here. No. That's terrible."

Maya says, without turning, "It's not terrible."

She taps her temple.

The helium hit her too. And for a year, she's been sneaking into his Friday dinosaur talks. Hours and hours of training.

She has heard him all night. Every single thought. The Saturn one. The dinosaur one. The eleven words.

Glint turns the colour of a fire engine.

Maya says, "By the way. Saturn would float in a bath. Your turn."
