What if you could taste words?

Someone says your name, and you taste strawberries. Someone says "Monday," and your mouth fills with burnt toast. Every conversation is a meal. And some of them are disgusting.

This sounds made up. It isn't. Some people really live like this. And by the end of this video, you'll discover that your brain does a tiny bit of it too.

<break time="1.0s" />

It's called synesthesia. It means your senses are blended together.

For most people, hearing is hearing and tasting is tasting. They stay in their own lanes. But in people with synesthesia, one sense automatically triggers another.

The most common type is seeing colors in letters and numbers. For some people, the letter A is always red. The number seven is always green. Every time. For their whole life.

Others hear music and see shapes floating in front of them. Some feel colors. Some taste sounds.

Scientists think a few percent of people have some type of synesthesia. Some musicians, like Billie Eilish, have talked about seeing colors when they make music.

But tasting words is one of the rarest types of all.

<break time="1.0s" />

One of the most famous people with it is a man in England named James Wannerton.

For James, words have tastes. Real tastes, that he feels in his mouth. And the strangest example he's given? The name Derek. He says it tastes like earwax.

Imagine sitting across from someone named Derek at dinner.

Scientists have tested people like James. They give them a list of words and write down the tastes they describe. Then, months or even years later, with no warning, they test them again.

And the tastes match. The same word, the same taste. You can't fake that kind of consistency.

<break time="1.0s" />

So let's give you this power.

You wake up, and your alarm goes off with a voice saying "Good morning." It tastes like warm pancakes. A good start.

You check your phone. A message from your boss. "Meeting." That one tastes like cold, soggy cereal. Of course it does.

You walk to the kitchen and your roommate says "Hey." Peppermint.

Every single word has a flavor. Every sentence is a mix. Your favorite song tastes amazing. The news... maybe not so much.

<break time="1.0s" />

And the weird thing is, the tastes often seem to come from childhood.

Scientists studying people who taste words noticed something. The tastes are usually things they ate as kids. Chocolate. Mashed potatoes. Cereal. Sweets. Not sushi or fancy coffee.

Researchers think these connections form when we're young, while the brain is still learning both words and foods. Once they're set, they stay for life.

And sometimes the taste follows the sound of the word. A word that sounds a bit like a food can end up tasting like that food. So names, places and brands that sound alike might share a flavor.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's where it gets really interesting.

You know that feeling when a word is on the tip of your tongue? You know it, but you just can't find it.

For people who taste words, the phrase "on the tip of your tongue" is almost literal. In one study, scientists showed them pictures of rare objects and asked them to name them. When they couldn't think of the word, some of them could already taste it.

The taste arrived before the word did.

Which tells scientists something amazing. The brain connects to the meaning of a word before it finds the actual sounds.

<break time="1.0s" />

But of course, living like this isn't always fun.

Imagine going on a date with someone whose name tastes like rotten eggs. Or having a teacher whose name tastes like soap. You'd hear it every day.

Imagine reading a menu where the word "salad" tastes like chocolate cake. Or a song that you love, but the chorus tastes terrible.

Some people say it can be distracting. Busy places with lots of talking can feel like a buffet you never ordered. But most people with synesthesia say they wouldn't give it up. To them, it's just how the world works.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, do you have a little bit of this too?

Let's test it. Imagine two shapes. One is round and soft, like a cloud. The other is sharp and spiky, like a star.

One is called "Bouba." The other is called "Kiki."

Which is which?

If you said the round one is Bouba and the spiky one is Kiki... you're with almost everyone. Scientists have found people all over the world, speaking very different languages, give the same answer.

Nobody taught you that. Your brain just linked the sound of a word to a shape. That's a tiny bit of blending between your senses.

You do it with taste too. People often describe a sound as "sweet," a voice as "smooth," or a color as "loud." Your senses are more mixed together than you think.

<break time="1.0s" />

So imagine a world where every word has a flavor. Where your best friend's name is your favorite dessert. Where poetry is a feast. And where you just have to hope your next boss isn't called Derek.
