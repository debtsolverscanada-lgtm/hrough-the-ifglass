First, here's something most people don't know.

Every living thing makes electricity.

Your heart beats because of electrical signals. Your muscles move because of electrical signals. Even your thoughts are electrical signals racing through your brain. That's how doctors can measure your heartbeat with stickers on your chest. They're picking up the electricity.

In water, that electricity spreads out into a faint electric field around your body. A very, very weak one.

And sharks can feel it.

<break time="1.0s" />

If you look closely at a shark's snout, you'll see lots of tiny dark pores. Each one leads to a little tube filled with a special jelly. At the end of the tube are sensors that detect electricity.

They're called the ampullae of Lorenzini. Named after the scientist who first described them, more than three hundred years ago.

Back then, nobody knew what they were for. Today we know they're one of the most sensitive electric detectors in nature. Sharks can sense electric fields measured in billionths of a volt.

<break time="1.0s" />

Scientists proved it with a clever experiment.

They buried a flatfish under the sand in a tank. A shark swam over, and found it instantly. Then they hid the fish inside a special chamber that let smells through, but blocked electricity. The shark swam right past it.

And when they buried electrodes in the sand that gave off a fish-like electric signal? The shark attacked the electrodes.

It wasn't smelling the fish. It was feeling it.

Sharks aren't the only ones with this power.

The platypus hunts with its eyes, ears and nose all shut tight underwater. It finds shrimp and worms by sweeping its bill side to side, feeling for their electric fields.

Some dolphins have this sense too. And bumblebees can sense the tiny electric charge on flowers. When a bee lands on a flower, it changes that charge a little. Scientists think other bees might notice the change, and skip that flower. Because someone already took the nectar.

Electricity is a secret language. And a lot of animals can read it.

And here's a strange side effect.

Because sharks are so sensitive to electricity, some devices try to keep sharks away by giving off powerful electric pulses. To a shark, it's like getting blasted with a horrible noise right in the face. It turns away.

And there are reports of sharks biting underwater internet cables. Nobody is completely sure why. One idea is that the electric and magnetic fields around the cables confuse them. Some companies wrap their cables in tougher material, just in case.
