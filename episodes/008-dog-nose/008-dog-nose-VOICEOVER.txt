What if you could smell like a dog?

You walk into a room, and you instantly know who was there. How long ago they left. What they had for lunch. Whether they were nervous.

A dog does this every single day. And by the end of this video, you'll realize your nose has been telling you a story your whole life. You just couldn't read it.

<break time="1.0s" />

First, how good is a dog's nose?

You have around five or six million smell receptors in your nose. A dog can have up to three hundred million. And the part of its brain that makes sense of smells is far bigger than yours, compared to the rest of its brain.

Some dogs can detect certain smells at incredibly low levels. Imagine one teaspoon of sugar, stirred into an enormous lake of water. And the dog can still tell it's there.

Dogs even breathe differently when they sniff. They take quick little sniffs, several times a second. And when they breathe out, the air leaves through the slits on the sides of their nose. So they don't blow away the smell they're trying to catch.

And each nostril smells separately. Which means a dog smells in stereo. It knows which direction a smell is coming from.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now let's give you that nose.

You wake up. Before you even open your eyes, you know what's going on in your house.

Someone made toast twenty minutes ago. Your roommate went out, and their smell is still hanging by the door. The cat walked across the kitchen counter. Again.

You open the fridge. You don't need to check the dates on anything. You can smell exactly which food is going off. Days before anyone else could.

And here's the big one. You can smell time.

<break time="1.0s" />

Smells fade. Slowly. So a fresh smell is strong, and an old smell is weak.

To you, that means a room has layers. The person who just walked in is a bright, loud smell. The person who left an hour ago is fainter. The person who visited yesterday is just a whisper.

Dogs use this to follow trails. In one study, tracking dogs could figure out which direction a person had walked, just by sniffing a handful of their footprints. Each step smells slightly weaker than the next one. So the dog follows the smell as it gets stronger.

With your new nose, you'd always know who came before you. Every bench in the park. Every door handle. Every seat on the bus. Your world would be full of invisible footprints.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now step outside.

And... it's a lot.

The garbage bins on your street. The exhaust from every car. The coffee shop. The bakery. Someone's perfume from across the road. Every dog that has ever lifted its leg on that lamp post.

The subway would be overwhelming. Hundreds of people, every one of them with their own smell. Their shampoo. Their breakfast. Their sweat.

Luckily, your brain has a trick. If you smell something for a while, it fades into the background. That's why you can't smell your own home, but you notice everyone else's. With a dog's nose, that trick would be working overtime.

<break time="1.0s" />

But it's not all garbage and sweat.

You'd be able to smell something nobody else can. Emotions.

When you're stressed, the chemicals in your sweat and breath change. In one study, dogs were trained to sniff samples of people's breath and sweat, taken before and after a stressful task. The dogs picked out the stressed samples with remarkable accuracy.

So with your new nose, you'd know when your friend is nervous before a job interview. You'd know when someone was lying to you and sweating about it. You'd smell fear.

<break time="1.0s" />

And it gets even more serious.

Dogs have been trained to smell certain diseases. Some dogs can warn their owners when their blood sugar is dropping dangerously low. In studies, dogs have sniffed out some cancers from samples of breath or urine.

And here's the craziest part. This isn't only dogs.

In Scotland, a woman named Joy Milne noticed her husband's smell had changed. A musky, oily smell. Years later, he was diagnosed with Parkinson's disease. Then Joy noticed that other people with Parkinson's had the same smell.

Scientists didn't believe her at first. So they tested her. They gave her T-shirts worn by people with and without Parkinson's. She got almost every one right. And the one she got "wrong"? That person was diagnosed with Parkinson's later.

A human nose, smelling a disease before doctors could find it.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's the twist.

Your nose is better than you think.

For a long time, people believed human noses were weak. But in one experiment, scientists laid a scent trail of chocolate across a grassy field. Then they blindfolded people, put them on their hands and knees... and asked them to follow it, like a dog.

Most of them could do it. And with practice, they got faster.

You've had a pretty good nose this whole time. You've just been looking at the world instead of smelling it.

And then you get home, and you can smell everything your family did today.
