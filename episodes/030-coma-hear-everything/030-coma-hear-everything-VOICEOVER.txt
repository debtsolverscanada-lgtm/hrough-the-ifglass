Glint is in a coma, and he can hear everything.

He can hear the heart monitor. He can hear Nan, holding his hand. And right now, he can hear a man at the end of his bed, whispering.

"Forty-eight hours. Then it's on a plane, and nobody will ever know."

Glint knows that voice. He heard it the night he fell.

The night that wasn't an accident.

And he can't move a single finger to warn anyone.

Let's go back. Three nights.

A storm is hammering the city science museum. Glint is on the night shift, up on the gallery above the Space Hall. Below him sits the museum's new star, the Kestrel Meteorite. A rock older than the Earth's oceans, on loan, worth millions.

At midnight, lightning hits. Every light dies.

In the dark, a torch beam moves across the meteorite case. Someone is humming. A pen clicks. Click, click. Click.

A man's voice says, "Nobody looks twice at a rock."

Glint steps back, quietly, and leans on the gallery railing.

The railing isn't there.

Darkness.

Then, slowly, a sound. Beep. Beep. Beep.

Here are the rules. And they're real.

Doctors measure how awake someone is with the Glasgow Coma Scale. Do your eyes open? Can you speak? Can you move when asked? The score runs from three to fifteen. Eight or less usually means a coma.

A doctor says, "Squeeze my hand, Glint."

Nothing.

"GCS six."

But in this world, Glint is wide awake inside a body that won't answer.

And real science says this happens more than anyone used to think. In two thousand and twenty-four, a study of more than three hundred patients found that about one in four who couldn't respond at all still showed signs of hidden awareness in brain scans.

And scans show that sound can still reach the brain of patients who can't respond. That's one reason doctors encourage families to keep talking to them.

So Glint can hear.

He just can't answer.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Nan.

Nan comes every day at nine. She reads him the football scores, badly. She tells him what she's baking. On Tuesday, she smuggles Mr. Pickles in inside her shopping bag. The nurses pretend not to see.

Here's a real fact. In a small study in two thousand and fifteen, patients with brain injuries heard recordings of their families telling familiar stories, four times a day for six weeks. They recovered faster than patients who heard silence.

So Nan keeps talking.

Minus: the question.

Every day, before she leaves, Nan asks the same thing. "Can you hear me, love?"

Glint screams inside. His mouth doesn't move.

He thinks of Jean-Dominique Bauby, a French magazine editor who woke up from a stroke unable to move anything except his left eyelid. He wrote a whole book by blinking, one letter at a time.

Glint tries to blink.

His eyelids don't even twitch.

Plus: Dr. Osei.

Glint's mentor sits with Nan and explains a famous experiment. In two thousand and six, scientist Adrian Owen asked a young woman who seemed completely unresponsive to imagine playing tennis. A brain area that plans movement lit up on the scanner. Then he asked her to imagine walking through her house. Totally different areas lit up. Exactly like a healthy volunteer.

Later, scientists turned it into a code. Imagine tennis for yes. Imagine your house for no.

Dr. Osei says, "I've booked the scanner. Friday, six in the morning."

Inside, Glint starts practising.

Minus: Kai's secret.

Kai visits every afternoon. Today, the room is empty, and his voice is shaking.

He says, "It should've been me. I swapped shifts with you that night, so I could go to a concert."

Then, quieter. "And I told Nan I'm at the bike shop. I'm not. I quit. I'm here."

Here's the thing. In brain scans of unresponsive patients, a familiar voice saying their own name can trigger a stronger response than a stranger's voice.

Glint's brain is shouting back. Nobody can see it.

Minus: the visitor.

Three in the morning. Footsteps. Flowers rustle.

A pen clicks. Click, click. Click.

It's Mr. Hale, the museum's new head of exhibitions. Very kind. Very worried.

He asks the night nurse, "Will he remember anything?"

The nurse says, "People often don't remember the moments right before a head injury."

Mr. Hale says, "Good."

Then he leans in close, and whispers, "Forty-eight hours. Then it's on a plane, and nobody will ever know."

Glint puts it together, lying perfectly still.

The meteorite in the case is a fake. The real one is packed in Mr. Hale's shipping crate, leaving the museum on Friday, at seven in the morning. And the railing didn't break. Someone had taken out the bolts to lift that crate up to the gallery.

Forty-eight hours.

That afternoon, the doctors talk to Nan in the corridor. If Friday's scan shows nothing, they'll start planning long-term care. A hospital two hundred miles away.

Nan says, "He's in there. I know he is."

Thirty hours.

Mr. Hale comes back with grapes. He tells Nan, "Such a tragic accident. Old railings." Nan thanks him. Glint's heart rate jumps. The monitor beeps faster. A nurse checks it. "Just a spike. It happens."

Twelve hours.

And now Glint sees the real problem. A scanner only answers yes or no, and only to the questions the doctors choose. Is your name Glint? Is it Tuesday? Nobody is going to ask, "Did someone steal a meteorite?"

He needs them to ask the right question.

Six hours.

Glint practises in the dark. Tennis. Serve, swing, the ball cracking off the racket. Yes. Then home. Nan's front door, the kitchen, the flour jar, Mr. Pickles asleep on the stairs. No.

Over and over.

One hour.

Six in the morning. The scanner is loud. A deep hammering knock, like being inside a drum.

A doctor's voice comes through the headphones. "Glint, if you can hear me, imagine playing tennis."

He swings with everything he has.

On the screen, a patch of his brain lights up.

Nan grabs Kai's arm.

The doctor asks, "Is your grandmother called Nan?" Tennis. Yes.

Then Kai leans into the microphone. "Did you fall because of me?"

Home. Front door. Kitchen. No.

Kai lets out a breath he's been holding for three days.

Dr. Osei frowns at the screen, and asks the smartest question in the room. "Glint, is there something we need to know?"

Yes.

"Is it about the fall?" Yes.

"Was it an accident?"

No.

Each answer takes minutes of imagining. The clock reads six forty.

"Is it about the museum? The meteorite?" Yes. Yes.

"Is the meteorite in the case real?"

No.

Dr. Osei is already on the phone.

At six fifty-eight, two police cars block the loading bay. Inside Mr. Hale's crate, wrapped in bubble wrap, is the real Kestrel Meteorite.

Four days later, Glint's left eyelid flickers.

A nurse brings an alphabet board, the same trick Bauby used. She points to letters, one by one, and he blinks at the right one.

The police want one thing. Proof that Mr. Hale visited at night. He swears he never came.

That's when Kai goes red, and admits the rest of his secret.

He didn't just quit his job. He got a new one. Night porter, at this hospital. So nobody could visit Glint without Kai knowing. He wrote every visitor down in a notebook.

Mr. Hale is in it. Every night. Three in the morning.

Then Nan sits down, takes Glint's hand, and asks one more time. "Can you hear me, love?"

Blink. Blink. Blink.
