It's three in the morning, and Glint is the only one awake.

He's always the only one awake. He hasn't slept in ten days. He doesn't need to.

But right now, his eyelids are heavy. His head is pounding. And downstairs, in the kitchen, Nan is slumped in her chair beside the old boiler, and she won't wake up.

Glint has a few minutes to figure out why.

<break time="1.0s" />

Let's go back. Ten days.

The museum is opening a new sleep exhibit. Glowing brain models. A giant dreaming whale. And a machine from an old sleep lab, a tangle of wires and dials that used to record people's brain waves through the night.

Glint is testing it on the night shift. Sensors stuck to his forehead. A storm outside. He presses the big green button.

The museum's old wiring surges. Every light in the hall flares.

The dials spin to zero. The machine sparks. And Glint feels it, like a window opening in his head. Fresh. Bright. Wide awake.

He waits for the tiredness to come back.

It never does.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules. And this is where the real science gets scary.

Feeling sleepy is mostly a signal. While you're awake, a chemical called adenosine builds up in your brain. The more it builds, the sleepier you feel. Coffee works by blocking that signal for a while.

In this world, Glint's power switches the signal off completely. He never feels sleepy.

But feeling sleepy was never the reason we sleep. It's just the alarm.

Sleep has real jobs. While you sleep, your brain replays the day and files it into long-term memory. And in experiments with mice, scientists found the spaces between brain cells widen by about sixty percent during sleep, so fluid can wash waste away faster. It's called the glymphatic system. Your brain's night-time cleaning crew.

Glint switched off the alarm. Not the need.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Time.

Most adults need seven to nine hours of sleep a night. That's about a third of your life. Glint suddenly has all of it back. He fixes Nan's wobbly table. He reads every book in the museum gift shop. He learns the piano. By night four, he can play Nan's favourite song.

Nan cries a little, then tells him to go to bed. He laughs. He doesn't have to.

Minus: The piano.

On night five, he sits down to play it again. And he can't. The notes are gone.

That's the filing job. Sleep is when your brain moves new skills and memories into long-term storage. Researchers have even watched it happen in rats. Brain cells that fired while a rat ran a maze fire again in the same order while it sleeps. Like a replay. Glint has no replay. Everything he learns is written in pencil, and it rubs out by morning.

Plus: Kai's challenge.

Kai, being Kai, decides to stay awake too. "Solidarity," he says. He makes it twenty-six hours before falling asleep face-first in a bowl of cereal.

Glint looks up the record. In nineteen sixty-four, a seventeen-year-old named Randy Gardner stayed awake for about eleven days for a school science fair. Researchers checked on him. By the end, he was moody, struggled to concentrate, and had trouble with memory. He even saw things that weren't there. Then he slept for about fourteen hours and recovered. Record books don't accept attempts anymore. It's considered too risky.

If nothing changes, Glint passes eleven days on Friday.

Minus: The gaps.

On day seven, Glint is pouring tea. He blinks, and the cup is overflowing. Two seconds are missing.

That's a microsleep. A tired brain can switch part of itself off for a few seconds, without asking. You don't feel it coming. You don't notice it going. It's one reason sleepy driving is so dangerous.

And Glint can't feel tired. So he never gets any warning at all.

Minus: Nan's question.

There is a very rare inherited illness called fatal familial insomnia, where the brain loses the ability to sleep. It's serious and heartbreaking, and it's one big reason scientists take sleep so seriously. Glint's power is fiction. But Dr. Osei reads him the research anyway, and asks him to come in for tests.

And Nan has started asking him the same small thing every morning. "Did you change the battery in the alarm by the boiler, love?"

"Yes, Nan," Glint says. Every morning.

He thinks he did. He really thinks he did.

<break time="1.0s" />

Which brings us back to three in the morning.

Glint has been reading in the kitchen. Nan is in her chair, dozing over her knitting, the way she does. Mr. Pickles is curled on the rug, and won't lift his head. The old boiler is humming.

And Glint feels something he hasn't felt in ten days.

Sleepy.

His eyelids sag. His head throbs. He's confused. He blinks, and the clock jumps forward a minute.

For a moment, he almost shrugs it off. Ten days awake. Of course he's foggy. Of course he has a headache.

But then he stops. And the scientist in him wakes up.

He can't feel sleepy. That's the rule. The alarm is switched off.

So if he feels sleepy... it isn't his brain.

It's the air.

Carbon monoxide. A gas with no colour and no smell. A faulty boiler can leak it. It slips into your blood and grabs the spots where oxygen should ride, and it holds on about two hundred times more tightly than oxygen does. It makes you sleepy, dizzy and confused. And people who are already asleep often don't wake up to notice it.

He looks up at the alarm by the boiler.

Its light is off. The battery is still in his coat pocket. Where it's been for ten days.

Nan won't wake when he shakes her. Her breathing is slow and shallow. Mr. Pickles barely mews.

And carbon monoxide is especially hard on a weak heart. Because the heart is a muscle that needs a constant supply of oxygen. Nan's heart is already working too hard.

The house is filling with something he can't see. And every minute, there's more.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now Glint knows what's happening. And he knows the rules that will try to stop him.

Every few seconds, his brain may switch off without warning. So he can't trust a long plan. He needs short steps. Steps that still work if he loses two seconds in the middle.

He says each one out loud, so he can't lose his place.

"Window."

He shoves the kitchen window wide open. Cold air pours in.

"Back door."

He kicks it open. A gap. Two seconds gone. He's on the floor, holding the doorframe. Keep going.

"Nan."

Crawling low helps in a smoky fire. But carbon monoxide mixes almost evenly with the air. Down low won't help. Out is the only answer. So he hooks his arms under Nan's and drags her, chair blanket and all, across the tiles and out into the freezing garden.

"Pickles."

The cat comes out under his arm, very annoyed. A good sign.

"Phone."

He dials. He says the words slowly and clearly. Carbon monoxide. Grandmother. Weak heart. Breathing. Then he sits in the frosty grass, holding Nan's hand, saying the address over and over so he won't lose it.

The operator tells him to stay outside, stay awake, and keep talking. For once, staying awake is the easy part.

The sirens come. Then the oxygen masks. A firefighter walks into the kitchen with a meter, looks at the reading, and goes very quiet. Then she turns the boiler off.

<break time="1.0s" />

Nan wakes up in the ambulance, furious about her nightie.

At the hospital, they give them both oxygen. And Glint finally sleeps.

He sleeps for fourteen hours. Just like Randy Gardner.

When he wakes up, he's tired. Gloriously, normally tired. The power is gone.

And then the strangest thing happens. He hums Nan's favourite song. And his fingers move on the blanket. Every note.

In this world, the memories were never lost. They were sitting in the pencil drafts, waiting for one long night of filing.

Nan says, "Next time, love, just take the nap."
