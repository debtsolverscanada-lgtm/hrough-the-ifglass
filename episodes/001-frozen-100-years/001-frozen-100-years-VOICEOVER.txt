What if you were frozen... and didn't wake up for one hundred years?

You close your eyes in this year. You open them in twenty-one twenty-six. Everyone you know is gone. Your phone is a museum piece. And the first doctor who sees you... isn't sure you should be alive.

So let's actually do it. Step by step. And find out what really happens.

<break time="1.0s" />

First problem. If we just put you in a freezer... you die.

Your body is about sixty percent water. And when water freezes, it doesn't stay neat. It grows sharp ice crystals. Millions of them. They spear through your cells like tiny glass knives, and they pull water out of everything around them. Your cells shrivel, then burst.

Thaw you out later, and you'd be a very cold, very dead person.

So scientists use a trick called vitrification. Instead of freezing you, they turn you into something closer to glass.

They replace the water in your blood vessels with a special antifreeze, called a cryoprotectant. Then they cool you down fast. So fast that the water molecules never get the chance to line up into crystals. They just... stop. Locked in place. No ice. No knives.

This isn't science fiction. It's how fertility clinics store human embryos. In twenty twenty, a baby girl named Molly Gibson was born from an embryo that had been frozen for twenty-seven years. Her older sister Emma was born three years earlier... from the very same batch of frozen embryos.

So small things can come back. The question is... can you?

<break time="1.0s" />

You're cooled to minus one hundred and ninety-six degrees Celsius. The temperature of liquid nitrogen. Colder than any natural temperature ever recorded on Earth's surface.

At this point, you are not asleep. You're not in a coma. Your heart isn't beating. Your brain shows no activity at all. Every chemical reaction in your body has slowed almost to zero.

Which means something strange. You don't age. Not a day. Not a second. One hundred years can pass, and your body stays exactly as it was... the moment you went under.

You're placed upside down in a steel tank called a dewar. Upside down, so if anything ever goes wrong... your brain is the last part to warm up.

And now... you wait.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's what most people never think about. For one hundred years, someone has to keep you cold.

Liquid nitrogen slowly boils away. So the tank has to be topped up, again and again. For a century. Through recessions. Power cuts. Wars. Companies going bankrupt.

And this has already gone wrong before. In the nineteen seventies, at a cemetery in California, a small cryonics group ran out of money. The tanks weren't refilled. And the people inside... thawed.

So your first danger isn't science. It's a bill nobody paid.

Let's say you get lucky. Your tank is kept cold, every single day... for one hundred years.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now comes the hardest part. Waking you up.

Rewarming is actually more dangerous than freezing. If you warm up too slowly, ice crystals form on the way back up, and do all the damage we just avoided. If you warm up unevenly, your outside expands while your inside is still glass... and you crack. Like an ice cube dropped into a warm drink.

But scientists have an idea. In twenty twenty-three, researchers at the University of Minnesota vitrified rat kidneys for up to one hundred days. Then they rewarmed them, using tiny iron nanoparticles that heat up evenly from the inside when you hit them with a magnetic field. They transplanted those kidneys into living rats... and they worked.

So in the future, doctors fill you with nanoparticles before freezing you. And one hundred years later, they switch on the field. Every cell warms up at the same time.

They flush out the antifreeze. They pump in warm blood. And then... they shock your heart.

<break time="1.2s" />

Thump.

<break time="0.8s" />

Thump.

<break time="0.8s" />

You're alive.

<break time="1.0s" />

The first thing you feel is cold. A deep, bone-level cold. Your body temperature is still well below normal, and you're shivering so hard your teeth hurt.

But here's a weird bonus. Because you didn't age, your muscles didn't waste away. Astronauts who spend six months in space come back weak and unsteady. You've been gone for one hundred years... and your legs are exactly as strong as the day you left.

Your brain, though, is a different story. Even with the best technology, some of your neurons didn't make it. You might lose some memories. You might forget the last few days before you were frozen. And for the first few hours... you have no idea where you are.

Then a nurse walks in. And she's wearing a mask.

<break time="1.0s" />

Because you are a walking biohazard.

Your immune system is one hundred years out of date. It has never met the viruses that will exist in twenty-one twenty-six. A flu that's harmless to everyone else in the building could put you in intensive care. And the germs living inside you are a century old. Some of them, nobody has seen in decades.

So before you see a single thing of this new world... you spend weeks in quarantine, getting vaccinated against diseases that didn't exist when you closed your eyes.

<break time="1.0s" />

Finally, they open the door.

What does the world look like? Nobody knows for sure. But we can make some educated guesses.

There are probably fewer people than when you left. United Nations projections show the human population peaking at around ten billion people in the twenty-eighties... and then slowly starting to shrink.

The sea is higher. Some coastal neighborhoods you remember may be behind sea walls. Or gone completely.

The people around you talk a little differently. Your English still works... but you'll hear words you don't know, and you'll say things that make people laugh. A hundred years ago, people said things like "the bee's knees." Imagine how you're going to sound.

The technology? Think about it this way. One hundred years before today, most homes didn't have a television. Nobody had ever been to space. Computers didn't exist. You, waking up in twenty-one twenty-six... are that person. Standing in front of things you don't have words for.

<break time="1.0s" />

Then there's the paperwork.

Legally, you were declared dead one hundred years ago. You don't exist. No identity. No bank account. No home.

But here's a twist. Let's say, before you went under, you put ten thousand dollars into an investment that earned seven percent a year. After one hundred years of compounding... that ten thousand dollars would be worth over eight million.

Of course, prices go up too. And a lawyer would have to prove that you... are actually you. Good luck with that. Your fingerprints are the same, but every photo of you is a century old.

Still. You might wake up a ghost... and a millionaire at the same time.

<break time="1.0s" />

But eventually, the excitement wears off. And you go looking for the people you left behind.

Your parents. Gone. Your friends. Gone. If you had children, they've lived entire lives, grown old, and died... while you lay in a steel tank, not aging a single second.

Maybe you find a great-great-grandchild. A stranger with your eyes. They know your name from a story. You know nothing about them at all.

Psychologists think this could be the biggest danger of all. Not the cold. Not the viruses. The loneliness. You'd be the only person on the entire planet who remembers the world you came from.

<break time="1.0s" />

So... could this really happen?

Right now, no one has ever been brought back from being cryogenically frozen. Not one person. Around five hundred people are lying in tanks of liquid nitrogen today, waiting for a future that may or may not be able to save them.

The very first, a psychology professor named James Bedford, was frozen in nineteen sixty-seven. He's still in his tank. Still waiting.

Maybe science will get there. Maybe one day, a door will open... and someone from our time will step out into a world that forgot them.
