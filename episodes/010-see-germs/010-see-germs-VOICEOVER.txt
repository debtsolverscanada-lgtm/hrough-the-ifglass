What if you could see every germ around you?

Your phone. Your toothbrush. Your kitchen sponge. The air right after someone sneezes. All of it, glowing with tiny living things.

First, a quick reality check.

Germs is a word we use for tiny living things that are too small to see. Mostly bacteria, fungi and viruses. A single bacterium is so small that dozens of them could line up across the width of one human hair. Viruses are even smaller. Much smaller.

So to see them, we're going to cheat. Let's give you germ vision. Bacteria look like tiny glowing dots. Viruses look like faint sparkles. And we'll turn it on.

<break time="1.0s" />

You open your eyes. And the first thing you see... is yourself.

You're glowing.

Your skin is covered in bacteria. Your mouth is full of them. Your gut has trillions. Scientists estimate there are around thirty-eight trillion bacteria living in and on your body. That's roughly the same as the number of human cells you have.

So, in a way, you're about half germ.

And here's the good news. Almost all of them are harmless. Many of them are helping you. The bacteria in your gut break down food you couldn't digest on your own. Some make vitamins. The ones on your skin take up space, so dangerous germs have a harder time moving in.

You're not a person covered in germs. You're more like a walking city. And most of the citizens are friendly.

Now let's walk around your house.

You head to the bathroom. You flush the toilet with the lid up. And you see something you'll never forget.

A cloud. Shooting up out of the bowl.

This is real. Scientists in Colorado used bright green lasers to light up the spray from a flushing toilet. Tiny droplets shot up and out of the bowl, reaching well above the seat within seconds. Your toothbrush might be sitting right in its path.

From now on, you close the lid.

Then you walk into the kitchen. And you look at the sponge.

It's not glowing. It's blazing.

A kitchen sponge is warm, wet, full of tiny holes and covered in food. It's paradise for bacteria. When scientists in Germany studied used kitchen sponges, they found some parts held tens of billions of bacteria in a space the size of a sugar cube.

Your kitchen sponge might be one of the most crowded places in your whole house.

Now you pick up your phone.

You touch it all day. After the bus. After the shopping cart. After lunch. And then you hold it against your face.

Some studies have found phones carrying more bacteria than a toilet seat. That's partly because toilet seats get cleaned, and phones almost never do.

With germ vision, your screen would look like a busy little galaxy. And you'd see a trail of glowing fingerprints everywhere you've touched.

Let's test the five-second rule.

You drop a slice of watermelon on the floor. You pick it up in under a second. Safe, right?

Scientists at Rutgers University tested exactly this. And they found that bacteria can jump onto food in less than one second. Wet food picked up germs fastest. Watermelon was one of the worst. Gummy candy picked up the least.

So the five-second rule isn't really a rule. But at least now you'd see exactly what you're eating.

Now step outside. And watch someone sneeze.

It's like a firework. A cloud of tiny droplets explodes out of their nose and mouth. The big ones fall to the ground quickly. But the smaller ones get carried in a warm, wet cloud that can travel several meters. Researchers at MIT filmed sneezes with high-speed cameras, and the cloud went much farther than anyone expected.

Everyone would start covering their mouth. Or sneezing into their elbow.

And there's something even stranger floating around you.

Every person gives off a personal cloud of bacteria. From their skin, their breath, their clothes. In one study, scientists sat people alone in a clean room and sampled the air around them. They could tell people apart, just from the bacteria floating around each person.

With germ vision, everyone you meet would have their own faint glowing aura. A cloud of microbes that's uniquely theirs.

So, would you start scrubbing everything with bleach?

Here's the twist. That could make things worse.

The vast majority of germs around you are harmless. Many are helpful. Your body is used to them. Your immune system is trained by them. If you wiped out every germ in your life, you'd also wipe out the friendly ones that keep the dangerous ones in check.

The real trick isn't killing everything. It's knowing where the dangerous ones hide. Wash your hands with soap for twenty seconds. Close the toilet lid. Replace that sponge.
