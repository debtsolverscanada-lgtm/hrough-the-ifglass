What if you saw the world in slow motion, like a fly?

A ball flies toward you, and it drifts through the air like a balloon. Someone tries to swat you, and you have all the time in the world to move. A movie looks like a slideshow.

There's a reason flies are so hard to catch. And by the end of this video, you'll realize that time itself might look different to every creature on Earth.

<break time="1.0s" />

First, how do you see movement?

Your eyes don't record the world like a video camera. But there is a limit to how fast they can keep up. If a light flickers fast enough, you stop seeing the flicker. It just looks like a steady glow.

For most people, that limit is somewhere around sixty flashes per second.

That's why a movie works. A movie is just a series of still pictures. Usually twenty-four of them every second. They change so fast that your brain blends them together into smooth motion.

But for a fly, that limit is much higher. Some flies can see flickers of around two hundred and fifty flashes per second. About four times faster than you.

So when you swing a rolled-up newspaper at a fly, it sees your hand coming in slow motion.

<break time="1.0s" />

And the fly is fast at reacting too.

Scientists filmed flies with high-speed cameras as something swooped toward them. In a fraction of a second, the fly figured out where the threat was coming from, adjusted its legs, and launched itself in the opposite direction. All before you'd even finished your swing.

That's not luck. That's a brain built for speed.

<break time="1.0s" />

So let's give you fly-speed vision.

You wake up. Your ceiling fan is spinning. But you can see each blade sweeping past, clearly, one by one.

You go to the kitchen and turn on the light. Some lights flicker very fast, too fast for normal eyes to notice. But for you... some of them are strobing. Like a nightclub that never ends.

You pour some water. And you can see the stream twist and break into droplets as it falls.

Outside, a hummingbird hovers by a flower. Normally its wings are a blur. But you can see them beating, back and forth.

<break time="1.0s" />

Now let's try sports.

A professional fastball reaches the plate in less than half a second. Batters have to decide whether to swing before the ball is even halfway there. Most of them are basically guessing, based on experience.

But you? You'd see the ball spinning. You'd read the seams. You'd have time to think.

Catching anything would be easy. Dodging anything would be easy. You'd be the best goalkeeper in the world.

<break time="1.0s" />

But there's a catch.

Slow motion means everything is slow. Including the boring stuff.

Waiting for the microwave. Sitting in traffic. A long lecture. Every moment would feel stretched out. A movie at the cinema would look jumpy and choppy, like a flip book. Some old TV screens would look like they were flashing.

And conversations? People's words would seem to crawl out of their mouths. Lips moving slowly. Pauses that feel like forever.

You'd get bored. A lot.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's the bigger idea.

Scientists have found that, in general, small animals with fast lifestyles see the world faster. Flies. Some birds. Small, quick hunters. Their eyes and brains update more often, so they can react in time.

Bigger, slower animals often update more slowly. Some turtles and other slow-moving creatures seem to see flicker at much lower speeds than we do.

So in a way, every creature lives in its own speed of time. To a fly, you're slow and clumsy. To a slow-moving turtle, you might look like you're on fast-forward.

<break time="1.0s" />

But hang on. Haven't you ever felt time slow down?

Maybe during a car crash, or a fall. People often say everything went into slow motion.

Scientists wanted to know if that's real. So they did something wild. They had volunteers fall backward from a tall tower into a safety net. While they fell, the volunteers wore a special watch on their wrist that flashed numbers too fast to read normally.

If time really slowed down, they should have been able to read the numbers.

They couldn't.

Time didn't actually slow down. But afterward, the volunteers said the fall felt longer than it was. The scientists think that in scary moments, your brain records memories more richly. So when you look back, the moment feels longer.

Your brain doesn't slow down time. It just remembers it in more detail.

<break time="1.0s" />

So imagine having fly vision for one day. You'd catch every ball. You'd see raindrops falling one by one. You'd watch a hummingbird's wings. And you'd sit through the longest, slowest boring meeting of your entire life.
