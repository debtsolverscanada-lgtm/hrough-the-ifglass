Every other person on Earth has vanished, and in six hours, the nuclear power plant on the river runs out of fuel for its cooling pumps.

Nobody is coming to fix it. There is nobody left to come.

Glint stands at the plant's open gate, alone, with a grumpy old cat zipped inside his backpack.

Six hours.

Behind those walls, a pool of water is the only thing standing between his city and a problem nobody could ever clean up.

Let's go back. Seven days.

It's three in the morning. Glint is on the night shift at the city science museum, down in the basement, fixing the old radio exhibit. The lights flicker once. His ears pop, like a plane coming in to land. Then nothing.

At seven, he walks outside.

Every alarm clock on the street is ringing. Nobody switches them off. No cars. No dog walkers. No one.

He runs to Nan's flat. Her bed is empty. Her glasses are on the bedside table. Mr. Pickles is yelling for his breakfast.

He calls Kai. It rings and rings. At the museum, Dr. Osei's coat still hangs on her chair.

By noon, the news websites have stopped updating. Every one of them. In every country.

Everyone is gone. Except Glint. And the cat.

<break time="1.0s" />

Here are the rules.

In this world, nobody knows why it happened. But real science can tell Glint exactly what happens next. Because the modern world doesn't run by itself. It runs on people. And when the people disappear, things fail in a very particular order.

Hour one. Phones still work. The internet still works. There's just nobody on the other end.

Then the power stations start to trip. A power grid has to match supply and demand every single second. Normally, people in control rooms do that balancing, all day and all night. With no one at the controls, the plants shut down, one by one. Experts think the grid would go dark within a day or two.

After that, the backup batteries die. Phone masts. Traffic lights. Hospital alarms. Silence.

And that order matters. Because the most dangerous things in a city are the ones that were built to be watched.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: the city is his.

Glint lies down in the middle of the motorway. He eats breakfast in the window of the fanciest restaurant in town. Mr. Pickles sleeps on the mayor's desk. Nobody tells either of them off.

Minus: the lights go out.

On the second night, the grid fails, exactly as the rules said. The fridges stop humming, and a new clock starts. A closed fridge keeps food safe for about four hours. A full freezer, about two days. After that, Glint lives on tins. The good news: canned food lasts for years.

Minus: the underground floods.

Cities sit on a lot of hidden water. In New York, pumps push about thirteen million gallons of water out of the subway every single day. When the power fails, the pumps stop. Glint walks down into his city's underground station with a torch. The bottom steps are already under water. Within days, the tunnels start to fill.

Plus: the stars.

With every light in the city dead, the sky comes back. The Milky Way stretches over the rooftops like spilled sugar. This really happened. In nineteen ninety-four, an earthquake blacked out Los Angeles before dawn, and people called the emergency services about a strange silver cloud in the sky. It was the Milky Way. Most of them had never seen it.

Glint lies on the museum roof with Mr. Pickles and names every constellation out loud.

Minus: the silence.

No cars. No voices. No Nan humming in the kitchen. Just wind and birds. Glint talks to the cat all day. The cat does not talk back.

Minus: the plant.

On day six, Glint finds an old leaflet in Dr. Osei's office. Riverside Nuclear Power Station. School open day. And one question stops him cold.

Who is looking after that?

<break time="1.0s" />

Here's what Glint knows. The reactor itself is fine. When the grid went down, it shut itself off within seconds, dropping control rods into the core. Nuclear plants are designed to do exactly that.

But next to the reactor, there's a pool. About twelve metres deep, holding the used fuel. Used fuel stays hot for years. Pumps keep the water cool, and the water keeps the fuel safe, and blocks its radiation.

Without the grid, those pumps run on emergency diesel generators. And in the United States, plants keep about seven days of diesel on site.

It's day seven.

Glint drives a museum van out to the plant at dawn. The gate is open. The control room is empty, its alarms blinking for nobody. The generator's fuel gauge reads six hours.

When it hits zero, the pumps stop. The pool starts to warm. In days, it boils. Over weeks, the water boils away. And if the fuel is ever uncovered, radiation could spread across the valley. Nan's flat. The museum. Kai's street. For a lifetime.

He could refill the diesel. But then again next week. And the week after. Forever. Alone.

Five hours.

He needs water that flows by itself. No power. No people.

Then he remembers Dr. Osei's favourite demonstration. A bucket on a high shelf. A hose. A bucket on the floor.

Gravity.

<break time="1.0s" />

Above the plant, behind the dam, sits the reservoir. Glint checks the map. In this story, its surface is forty metres higher than the pool.

Here's the real science. Water pushes harder the higher it starts. Every ten metres of height adds about one more atmosphere of pressure. That's how water towers work. No pumps. Just height.

And after the Fukushima accident in two thousand and eleven, nuclear plants added emergency connections, so water could be fed into the fuel pool from outside, through hoses.

Glint finds the plant's fire hoses. Hundreds of metres of them. He drags them up the hill, from the dam's outlet valve down to the pool connection, length by length, clicking each one together.

Three hours. One hour. His arms are shaking.

Ten minutes.

The generator coughs. Sputters. Stops.

The pumps wind down.

Glint sprints up to the dam and heaves the valve open. Nothing. Then the hose kicks like a living thing. It swells and stiffens, all the way down the hill.

Inside, water pours into the pool. Deep below the surface, the old fuel glows a soft, eerie blue. That's real too. It's called Cherenkov light.

The level holds. Cold water flows in, and warm water spills out through the overflow. No power. No people. Just gravity, which never takes a day off.

Glint sits on the pool deck and cries. Mr. Pickles headbutts his chin.

<break time="1.0s" />

Months pass.

The city turns wild. Weeds burst through the pavement. Foxes sleep in the shopping centre. A deer wanders into the museum and eats the fern display. Scientists have seen this before. In the Chernobyl exclusion zone, after people left in nineteen eighty-six, wolves, elk and wild boar came back in huge numbers.

Every night, Glint switches on one lamp in the museum's top window, powered by the solar panels from the energy exhibit. Just in case.

Month six. The first snow. The last leaves fall from the trees.

And across the city, a window lights up.

By the rules, that's impossible. Every battery died months ago. That building has no solar panels. A light means a generator, and a generator needs someone to fill it.

All summer, the new forest hid Glint's lamp. Now the trees are bare.

The far window blinks. Once. Twice. Three times.

Glint grabs his torch and blinks back. Three times.

A long pause. Then the far light starts to flash in Morse code. Slow. Careful. Glint knows Morse from the old radio exhibit, the one he was fixing the night it all began. He writes each letter in the frost on the glass.

P. I. C. K. L. E. S.

There is only one person in the whole world who would ask about that cat.

Nan.
