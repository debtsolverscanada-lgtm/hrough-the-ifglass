Nan has nine seconds.

A delivery lorry, twelve tonnes of it, is sliding down icy Hill Street with no brakes. At the bottom, Nan is stepping onto the crossing. Mr. Pickles in his carrier. A tin of cinnamon buns under her arm.

Nine seconds.

But for Glint, nine seconds lasts fifteen minutes.

And he is very, very hungry.

Let's go back. One week.

Three in the morning at the city science museum. A storm is rattling the roof. Glint, gold glasses, mint scarf, is up a ladder inside the great clock exhibit, oiling a pendulum the size of a door.

Lightning hits the tower.

The lights die. Something hums through the brass, into his hands, into his chest.

When he opens his eyes, the pendulum is hanging in the air. Not stopped. Moving. Just impossibly slowly.

A raindrop drifts past the window like a soap bubble.

The world hasn't slowed down.

Glint has sped up. A hundred times.

<break time="1.0s" />

Dr. Osei spends the next night testing him. And these are the rules.

One second for the world is one hundred seconds for Glint. His muscles, his thoughts, his heartbeat, all running a hundred times faster. It switches on when his heart races. And it switches off when he lets go.

Sound becomes useless. A voice is a vibration in the air, and stretched out a hundred times, it drops into a rumble far too deep for human ears. So when Glint speeds up, the world goes silent.

Light is different. Light travels about three hundred thousand kilometres every second, almost a million times faster than sound. So he still sees everything, instantly.

Then there's the air. The faster you move, the harder air pushes back. Go twice as fast, and the push is four times stronger. If Glint sprinted at full power, he'd blast past the speed of sound.

So Dr. Osei gives him one rule. Walk. Never sprint.

<break time="1.0s" />

Plus: Glint is never late again.

Monday morning, Nan switches the kettle on. A kettle takes about three minutes to boil. For Glint, that's five hours. He makes breakfast, scrubs the oven, fixes the dripping tap, and reads half a library book. The kettle clicks. Nan stares at her spotless kitchen and says, "Who are you, and what have you done with my grandson?"

Minus: his shoes.

Every step at his speed scrapes the ground a hundred times faster, and friction turns movement into heat. By Tuesday, his trainers smell like a burning tyre. By Wednesday, the soles are gone. Nan holds up the fourth melted pair and says, "Shoes don't grow on trees, love."

Plus: nothing falls anymore.

Mr. Pickles shoves Nan's favourite glass off the counter, as usual. A glass dropped from counter height hits the floor in less than half a second. For Glint, that's more than forty seconds. He strolls across the kitchen, catches it, and puts it back. Mr. Pickles looks deeply offended.

Minus: the hunger.

A young man burns around two and a half thousand calories a day. But Glint's body is running a hundred times faster, so one hour of speed burns food like a hundred hours of living. He eats twelve sandwiches at lunch. He faints in the dinosaur hall. And Nan, who bakes when she's worried, starts baking day and night.

Plus: the hidden world.

In the museum garden, a honeybee hovers over the lavender. Its wings beat more than two hundred times a second. Normally, just a blur and a buzz. Glint watches every single beat, slow and perfect, like a dancer.

Minus: the boom.

Kai, of course, has a terrible idea. Saturday night, an empty car park. Kai says, "One proper sprint. For science."

Glint sprints. He passes the speed of sound, and the air in front of him piles up into a shock wave. A sonic boom. Every window in the chip shop shatters.

Nobody is hurt. Kai says it was the best night of his life. Glint pays for the windows out of his wages.

And Dr. Osei adds the hardest rule of all. Never grab a person at your speed. Yanking someone a few metres in the blink of an eye is like hitting them with a car. Sudden acceleration hurts real bodies.

Minus: Glint can't save people the way heroes do in films.

And on Sunday morning, he finds out what that means.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint is on the museum steps when he sees it, and his heart races, and the power switches on by itself. Raindrops freeze. Sound vanishes.

At the top of Hill Street, a delivery lorry has hit black ice. The driver's face is frozen in panic, his foot jammed on a brake that isn't working. Twelve tonnes, sliding.

At the bottom: Nan. On the crossing. Coming to surprise him with buns. She's looking down at Mr. Pickles, mid-hiss inside his carrier. She hasn't even seen the lorry.

Glint does the maths. Nine seconds until the lorry reaches her. Fifteen minutes, his time.

He can't shout. She'd never hear him.

He can't push the lorry. Twelve tonnes on the move carries enormous momentum. He'd bounce off it like a fly off a bus.

He can't grab Nan. At his speed, yanking her clear would hurt her as badly as the lorry.

And his stomach is screaming. He skipped breakfast. His legs are shaking, and the world is starting to flicker, slipping in and out of normal speed. If his fuel runs out, the power dies, and nine seconds becomes nine seconds again.

Fourteen minutes.

He looks around. Rain. Railings. Parked cars. And beside the crossing, behind a roadworks barrier, a mountain of loose gravel.

Thirteen minutes.

His hands are trembling now. Every second he stands here thinking, his body is burning fuel he doesn't have.

Glint remembers a poster from the museum's transport gallery. Runaway lorry ramps.

<break time="1.0s" />

On steep mountain roads, there are escape lanes filled with deep, loose gravel. When a lorry's brakes fail, it steers in, the wheels sink, and the gravel drags it to a stop. All that energy goes into shoving stones aside.

Friction destroyed his shoes all week. Now friction is going to save Nan.

First, fuel. He opens Nan's tin and eats six cinnamon buns. Sorry, Nan.

He feels the sugar hit. The flickering steadies. Six buns, roughly two thousand calories. Enough to keep his body going for the next fifteen minutes of hard work, his time. Just enough.

Then he grabs a shovel from the roadworks. Walk, never sprint. Shovel after shovel, he builds a deep bed of gravel across the lorry's lane, curving away from the crossing. Eleven minutes. Seven. Four. His shoes smoke. His arms burn.

He reaches into the cab and turns the steering wheel, slowly, toward the gravel.

Last, Nan. He doesn't grab her. He takes her arm and leans, gently, for three long minutes of his time. To the world, Nan simply takes two steps back onto the pavement.

He lets go. Sound crashes back in.

The lorry's wheels hit the gravel and sink. Stones spray. Metal groans. And twelve tonnes of lorry crunch to a stop, one metre from where Nan was standing.

<break time="1.0s" />

Glint collapses on the kerb. Empty. Shaking. Barefoot. He's sure the power is gone, burned out for good.

Nan sits down beside him and opens the tin. Six buns missing.

She doesn't look surprised.

Nan says, "Melted shoes every night. An empty fridge every morning. You think I didn't notice?"

That's why she's been baking all week. Not because she was worried.

Because she knew. She was keeping him fuelled.

Then she squeezes his hand and says, "Your grandad used to eat like that too, you know."

<break time="1.0s" />

So, would you take this power? A hundred times faster than everyone else, but a hundred times hungrier, a pair of shoes a day, and a world with no sound?
