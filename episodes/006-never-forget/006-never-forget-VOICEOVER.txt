What if you could never forget anything?

Every birthday. Every conversation. Every embarrassing thing you've ever said. Perfectly. Forever.

It sounds like a superpower. And a few dozen people on Earth really live like this. But by the end of this video, you might decide that forgetting... is the real superpower.

<break time="1.0s" />

First, let's see how much you normally forget.

Try this. What did you eat for lunch eleven days ago? What did you talk about with your best friend three Tuesdays ago?

Most people have no idea. And that's completely normal. Over a hundred years ago, a scientist named Hermann Ebbinghaus tested how fast we forget. Within a day of learning something new, a huge chunk of it is already gone. Within a week, most of it.

Your brain isn't broken. It's cleaning. It keeps what seems important, and quietly throws the rest away.

Now imagine that cleaning just... stopped.

<break time="1.0s" />

In two thousand and six, scientists in California published a case about a woman named Jill Price.

Give her any date from her teenage years onward, and she could tell you what day of the week it was. What she did. What the weather was like. What was on the news.

Not because she studied it. She just remembered. Automatically. Every single day.

Scientists called it highly superior autobiographical memory. Since then, they've found only a few dozen people like her. And when you hear how they describe it, it doesn't sound like a gift.

Jill Price described her memory as non-stop, uncontrollable, and totally exhausting. Like a movie running in her head that never ends. Like a split screen, where she's living in the present while her past plays on the other side.

<break time="1.0s" />

So let's give you this power. And see what happens.

You wake up tomorrow morning. And everything is there.

Your first day of school. The smell of your grandparents' house. The exact words your teacher said when you got a question wrong in third grade. Not fuzzy. Sharp. Like it happened this morning.

At first, it's amazing. You never lose your keys. You never forget a name. You remember every joke, every song lyric, every birthday.

Your friends start testing you. "What did we do on New Year's, five years ago?" And you tell them. The food. The music. Who left early. What time it was when the power went out.

You'd win every argument. Because you'd remember exactly what everyone said.

And that's where the problems start.

<break time="1.0s" />

Because you'd also remember every argument you've ever lost.

Every mean comment. Every breakup. Every time someone let you down. Most people's bad memories slowly lose their sting. Scientists have even measured it. Over time, the painful feeling attached to a memory usually fades faster than the good feeling attached to a happy one. Time really does heal.

But not for you.

When you remember a bad day, it doesn't feel like an old photo. It feels like reliving it. The embarrassment is just as hot. The sadness is just as heavy. Ten years later.

People with this kind of memory have described how hard it is to let go of a grudge. Not because they want to hold on. Because the memory refuses to fade.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's something surprising.

You'd expect people with perfect memory to be geniuses at school. They're not.

When scientists tested them on things like memorizing lists of words, or remembering faces, many of them did about as well as everyone else. Their superpower works on their own life. Their experiences. Not on textbooks.

So you'd still have to study for your exams. You'd just remember exactly how stressed you felt while studying.

<break time="1.0s" />

There's an even older case. Almost a hundred years ago, a Russian psychologist studied a man known simply as S.

S could remember enormous tables of numbers, and recall them perfectly years later. But he struggled with something most people find easy. Understanding the big picture. He got so lost in the details that it was hard for him to follow a story, or understand a metaphor.

His memory was so full, he tried to force himself to forget. He even wrote things down on paper and burned it. Hoping the memory would go up in smoke too.

It didn't.

<break time="1.0s" />

And here's the strangest twist of all.

Even people with this incredible memory can be fooled.

In one study, scientists tried to plant false memories. Little details that never actually happened. And the people with super memory fell for it about as often as everyone else.

Because memory isn't a video recording. Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it. A little differently each time. Even the best memory on Earth is still a story your brain is telling you.

<break time="1.0s" />

So why do we forget at all?

Because forgetting is useful.

If you remembered every single time you saw a dog, you might struggle to understand what "dog" means. Forgetting the details lets your brain see the patterns. It turns a thousand experiences into one simple idea.

Forgetting lets you move on from a mistake. It lets you forgive people. It lets an old heartbreak become just a story you tell.

Your brain isn't failing when it forgets. It's protecting you.

<break time="1.0s" />

So imagine it's twenty years from now, and you still remember everything. Every word your parents ever said to you. The good ones, and the bad ones. Every face. Every moment you wish you'd done differently.

You'd never lose a single memory of the people you love.

But you'd never be able to escape the moments that hurt you either.
