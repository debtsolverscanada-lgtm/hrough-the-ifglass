What if you had nine brains, like an octopus?

One in your head. And one in each arm. Your hands could make their own decisions. Taste the world. Reach for things before you even think about it.

It sounds like science fiction. But an octopus lives like this every day. And by the end of this video, you'll find out that you already have a second brain inside you.

Let's meet the octopus.

An octopus has around five hundred million nerve cells. That's more than a mouse, and in the same league as a dog. But here's the weird part. Most of those nerve cells aren't in its head.

About two thirds of them are in its arms.

Each arm has its own cluster of nerve cells, running the arm's movements. That's why people say an octopus has nine brains. One central brain, and a mini brain in each of its eight arms.

The central brain gives an order, like "go get that crab." And the arm figures out the rest. How to bend. How to grab. Which sucker to use.

<break time="1.0s" />

And octopus arms don't just touch. They taste.

The suckers on an octopus's arms are packed with sensors that detect chemicals. When an octopus slides an arm into a crack in a rock, it's tasting everything it touches. It can feel and taste a crab hiding in the dark before it ever sees it.

Imagine eating with your hands... and actually tasting your food with your fingertips.

<break time="1.0s" />

So let's give you an octopus body. Well, just the arms.

You wake up. You reach for your phone. And your arm finds it before you even open your eyes. It doesn't need you to look. It just knows.

You make breakfast. While your head is thinking about your day, your left arm is buttering toast and your right arm is pouring coffee. Your arms are handling the details. You just give the orders.

You could text and cook at the same time. You could tie your shoes, fix your hair and answer an email, all at once. Every arm working like its own little assistant.

<break time="1.0s" />

But there are some problems.

Remember, your arms can taste. Which means every doorknob, every subway pole, every shopping cart handle... you're tasting it.

Shaking hands with someone would be a very personal experience.

And then there's the question of who's in charge. Octopus arms can act on their own. In experiments, an arm that was separated from the octopus kept reacting to touch for a while. It could still reach, and still grab.

So imagine you're on a diet. Your head says no to the cookie. But your arm has already tasted it on the plate... and it's reaching.

<break time="1.0s" />

There's a mystery scientists still wonder about. How does an octopus keep its eight independent arms from getting tangled up? Or grabbing each other?

One study found a clever trick. Octopus suckers grab almost anything automatically. But there seems to be a chemical in the octopus's own skin that tells the suckers: don't grab this, it's me.

Your arms would need the same rule. Otherwise, you'd spend your whole day getting stuck to yourself.

<break time="1.0s" />

The octopus has other strange powers too.

It has three hearts. Its blood is blue. It can change the color and texture of its skin in a fraction of a second, to blend in with rocks and coral.

And here's the twist. Octopuses are thought to be colorblind. Their eyes have only one type of light sensor. So how do they match colors so perfectly?

Nobody knows for sure. But scientists have found light-sensing proteins in octopus skin. Some think the skin itself might be sensing light. Seeing, without eyes.

<break time="1.0s" />

So, could a human ever think like this?

Here's the surprise. You already do. A little.

Inside your gut, there's a huge network of nerve cells, wrapped around your stomach and intestines. Hundreds of millions of them. Roughly as many as an octopus has in its whole body.

Scientists call it your second brain.

It runs your digestion by itself. It doesn't need your head to tell it what to do. Even if the main nerve connecting it to your brain is cut, your gut keeps working on its own. And it's constantly sending signals up to your brain. That's part of why you get butterflies in your stomach when you're nervous.

And your spinal cord does its own thinking too. When you touch something hot, the signal to pull your hand away can come from your spinal cord, before your brain has even noticed the pain.

You're not one single mind sitting in your head. You're a team.

<break time="1.0s" />

But here's the sad part about octopuses.

For all their intelligence, most octopuses live only a year or two. They can solve puzzles, open jars, and escape from aquarium tanks. And then they're gone.

Imagine what they could do if they lived as long as we do.
